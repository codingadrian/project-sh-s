# A Structured Reference Grammar of Boruca (Brunka)

*Compiled from eight academic sources for language-revival and pedagogical use*

---

## 1. Overview of Boruca / Brunka

Boruca (also called Brunka or Brúnkajk) is a Chibchan language of the southern Pacific region of Costa Rica, historically spoken in the area of Buenos Aires, Puntarenas province, near the Panama border, in two reserves: Boruca and Curré (Castro 2010, "The Boruca Verbal Dimension"; Castro 2010, "The Complex Clause in Boruca"). Genetically, Boruca belongs to the Isthmian branch of the Southern Chibchan languages, alongside Teribe and Cuna, and — like those two — has no immediate sister language, which some scholars take as a sign that it may constitute a branch of its own within Chibchan (Castro 2012, citing Quesada 2007). Other Isthmian-branch relatives spoken in the region include Bribri and Cabécar (Viceita branch) and Guaymí/Ngäbere and Bocotá (Guaymian branch); the now-extinct Chánguena and Dorasque belonged to the Doracic branch (Quesada 2007, cited in Castro 2012).

Boruca is functionally extinct as a spoken first language. Sources across the corpus disagree slightly on the precise timeline, which itself is informative for revival planning: Quesada Pacheco (1997–98) describes roughly five fluent speakers and about thirty semi-speakers still able to produce isolated phrases in the mid-1990s; by Castro's 2010 account, the last fully native speaker died in 2003, leaving a single remaining semi-speaker (whose semi-speaker spouse died in 2007). Quesada Pacheco (2011, "La causatividad en el boruca") likewise describes the language, as of that writing, as known only passively by a few adults. All current descriptive work therefore rests on a combination of (a) legacy field materials — most importantly Pittier's 1892–96 manuscripts (published 1941) and Constenla & Maroto's *Leyendas y tradiciones borucas* (1979/1986) — and (b) elicitation with the last semi-speakers, notably Nemesio González (used by Castro) and doña Paulina Leiva, Espíritu Santo Maroto, Isolina Céspedes, and Ernesto González (the four narrators whose traditional stories underlie Abarca González's frequency study). Revitalization efforts by the Costa Rican Ministry of Public Education, going back to the 1970s and including Quesada Pacheco's own teaching grammar *Hablemos [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)* (1995) and school materials (1998), are acknowledged by their own author as having had limited success in producing active speakers, which is part of the motivation for the more recent linguistic documentation this grammar draws on.

**A note on scope (2026 addition).** This grammar covers spoken/written Boruca (Brúnkajk) only. A related but linguistically distinct system, **Brunca Sign Language**, is used within the same community by a small group of deaf and hearing signers; per its one dedicated study (Woodward 1992), it is not structurally or lexically derived from spoken Boruca, so it is documented separately in `boruca_sign_language.md` rather than folded into this grammar.

## 2. Phonology & Orthography Notes

> **Project orthographic standard.** For all materials produced under this revival project, the glottal stop /ʔ/ is written with the **apostrophe (')**, not \<v\> or any other symbol. See §2.1 for the rationale and supporting evidence.

Phonological documentation across these sources is thin — none of the eight papers is a dedicated phonological description, and this is a genuine gap for revival material (see §8). What can be gleaned:

- **Practical alphabet.** Quesada Pacheco (2011) describes the "official practical alphabet" used in modern Boruca texts as largely following Spanish orthographic conventions, with two notable exceptions: **\<sh\>** represents the postalveolar fricative /ʃ/, and **\<v\>** represents the glottal stop /ʔ/. The velar nasal consonant /ŋ/ is written **\<n̈\>** (a tilde-n), though other publications have used the Greek letter eta (η) for the same sound when the tilde-n was not typographically available.
- **A second alphabet chart** appears in Abarca González's frequency study (based on Constenla & Maroto's practical alphabet), giving a fuller consonant inventory organized by place and manner: bilabial, dental/alveolar, palatal, velar, and laryngeal stops, affricates, fricatives, a simple vibrant/flap, and nasals; the vowel inventory is the five-vowel system /i, e, a, o, u/ (high i/u, mid e/o, low a).
- **Tone.** Boruca has a **two-way tonal contrast**, high and low, represented in the practical alphabet by an acute accent (´) for high tone and its absence for low tone (Abarca González; also referenced by Quesada Pacheco 2011 in connection with tonal harmony, see §5.3). Quesada Pacheco (2011) notes that Boruca shows **tonal harmony**, whereby a verbal suffix takes on a high tone when the monosyllabic verb root has low tone, and vice versa (Quesada Pacheco 1985, cited therein).
- **Suprasegmental causative alternation.** Directly relevant to phonology-morphology interaction: certain monosyllabic low-tone verbs become causative/transitive when the root shifts to high tone (see §5.3 below) — apparently a typologically unique mechanism not otherwise attested in the Chibchan family per Quesada Pacheco's comparison.
- **Morphophonemic deletion.** Castro (2012) notes that the infinitive marker **-í / -íh** is deleted when TAM markers are added to the verb stem, and that the root-final /k/ of *ték* ('to talk') is lost before the past-tense marker *-krá* (i.e., *tegrá* rather than *tekrá*) because speakers reanalyze that /k/ as belonging to the tense morpheme rather than the root (Quesada & Quesada Pacheco 1995).

Beyond these scattered notes, there is no systematic phonemic inventory, no discussion of syllable structure, minimal pairs, or stress, and no phonetic description of vowel or consonant realization in the corpus. Any revival-oriented phonology will need to draw directly on Quesada Pacheco's teaching materials (*Hablemos [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)*, 1995) or Constenla & Maroto (1986), neither of which was among the source texts read for this synthesis.

### 2.2 A historical-comparative phonology, newly folded in (2026 addition)

Quesada Pacheco's "La lengua boruca en manuscritos del siglo XIX" (*Estudios de Lingüística Chibcha* 13, 1994) is, in fact, the dedicated phonological study the paragraph above says doesn't exist in this corpus — it was simply never read for this document before now. It builds a comparative phonology by cross-referencing the four 19th-century wordlists (Valentini 1862, 147 entries; Gabb 1875, 250 entries; Thiel 1882, ~1,100 entries plus an unpublished 93-entry glossary; Pittier 1892–96/1941, ~1,250 entries) against modern Boruca as documented in the rest of this project's corpus. Findings of direct relevance here:

- **Pittier's own phonetic chart**, reproduced in full in the source, is the only first-hand 19th-century phonetic description of Boruca that exists. Pittier (writing from a French-speaking frame of reference) mistakenly posited nasalized vowels in forms like *teng* 'to cure', *ung* 'fish' — Quesada Pacheco confirms this is an artifact of Pittier's French phonetic training, not a real Boruca vowel-nasality contrast; no source, historical or modern, otherwise attests nasal vowels in the language.
- **A documented phonemic merger in progress.** The clearest and most citable finding: 19th-century transcriptions distinguish two sound sequences, a bilabial-stop-plus-palatal-fricative [pʃ] (Valentini *tx*, Gabb/Pittier *ksh*/*px*/*gx*, Thiel *ch*/*psh*) and a velar-stop-plus-palatal-fricative [kʃ] — both distinct from plain /ʃ/ — in words like *pʃék* 'turtle' (mod. */ʃék/*), *pʃúʔlk* 'toad' (mod. */ʃúʔk/*), *kʃiʔskáŋ* 'five' (mod. */ʃiʔkáŋ/*). By the modern period these have fully merged into /ʃ/, with the stop element lost. This confirms the same merger already noted briefly in `boruca_historical_periods.md` Period 2 ("the /pʃ/~/kʃ/ to /ʃ/ merger were already unstable or in transition by the 1890s") but with the actual comparative evidence behind it now available here.
- **Confirmation that Boruca never had /l/.** Pittier explicitly states Boruca lacks /l/ despite including the letter in his chart (apparently by template habit); Valentini and Gabb do record /l/ in some entries, but Quesada Pacheco argues these are Spanish-contact forms, not evidence of a native liquid phoneme — modern /l/-containing Boruca forms are analyzed the same way (Spanish loans). This directly supports and dates the b/d/g-devoicing and /k/-deletion-before-pause processes already partially documented via Constenla & Maroto (1979) elsewhere in this grammar: the 19th-century sources show these processes already underway but not yet complete (e.g. */bóʔk/* 'young man' appears both with and without the final /k/ across sources), giving a rough terminus post quem of "in progress, not complete" for the 1860s–1890s.
- **An etymology worth flagging for `boruca_word_formation.md`**: modern */kagtú/* 'devil' derives transparently from two historically separate morphemes still visible in the 19th-century forms (Valentini *kabru*, Gabb *kag brú*, Thiel *[ca](dictionary.html?q=ca) buru*, Pittier *kag bru*) — */ká/* 'day, time' + */brú/* 'chief, leader' (*brú* itself now obsolete as a free form) — a compound now felt by modern speakers as a single unanalyzable root, since the free-standing form *brú*/*buru* has otherwise dropped out of the language. This is a clean, citable example of compound-to-simplex reanalysis (opacification) via loss of one component's independent currency — a word-formation pathway not otherwise exemplified in this project's corpus.
- **Pretonic vowel reduction and tone-conditioned vowel alternation** (e~i alternation under low tone, e.g. *ktiéxti* → Pittier *kri-eq-*, *okláti* → *ok-xa-ri*) are richly attested across the 19th-century sources and match patterns Quesada Pacheco says are "not entirely unknown" in modern narratives and in Maroto's own unpublished notebooks — a genuine point of continuity worth cross-referencing against this project's own Maroto Rojas transcriptions (`boruca_maroto_rojas_full_text.md`) in any future phonology work.

See `boruca_manuscritos_1994_synthesis.md` for the fuller treatment, including the morphological, syntactic, and lexical-semantic findings from the same source (§4.3 above covers its findings on the *ki* clitic specifically), and for the significant follow-up opportunity the source itself represents: it reproduces Pittier's three bilingual texts (the earliest connected Boruca narrative material known, 1892–96) fully re-transcribed and re-glossed in modern orthography, and includes an alphabetized comparative historical vocabulary of roughly 1,700 entries across all four 19th-century sources — neither has yet been mined for this project's vocabulary database.

### 2.1 Orthography standardization: the glottal stop

**The problem.** No single symbol for the glottal stop is used consistently across the sources, or in current community usage. This is not a minor typographic detail — the glottal stop is phonemically load-bearing in Boruca (it distinguishes words and, per §2's tonal-harmony and morphophonemic notes, interacts with tone and suffix boundaries), so an unstable notation makes both the academic corpus and any new material harder to read and to search.

**Evidence of the inconsistency:**

- *Within the formal academic literature itself.* Quesada Pacheco (2011, "La causatividad en el boruca") gives \<v\> as the practical-alphabet symbol for /ʔ/. But his own earlier work does not follow this: "El adjetivo boruca" (1997–98) glosses examples with the **apostrophe** throughout — *[anté'shi](dictionary.html#e-2083)* 'leaned', *bi'shi* 'buried', *cáchi'shi* 'covered', *tutsi'* 'stretched' — and the separately sourced 2,255-entry vocabulary spreadsheet (*bruncajc-vocabulary.xlsx*) likewise uses the apostrophe as its working notation (*[a'](dictionary.html#e-2100)* 'to lower', *[a'chí'](dictionary.html#e-2101)* 'decaído, débil', *[a'chí'ua](dictionary.html#e-2102)* 'bordón'). In other words, the apostrophe is already the de facto standard in the actual linguistic data, independent of what the cited "official" alphabet chart says.
- *In current community usage (see the social-media examples reviewed with the project owner, June 2020–October 2025).* Boruca-affiliated pages and users write the same sound, or a closely related one, with a variety of ad hoc, keyboard-typable symbols rather than \<v\>: a caret (**^**) in forms like *Cagrú^rójc* (Kabrú Rójc Brunkajc Boruca, June 2023), *Oc^sa* for 'Osa' (Saúl Morales, October 2025), *yubú^* and *[rít](dictionary.html?q=r%C3%ADt) co^* (comment thread on Gilbert González Maroto's post, March 2020); and a straight double-quote in *Brun"kajc* (Patrimonio Brunka, June 2020). None of these community authors appear to be using \<v\>, and no two of them use exactly the same symbol as each other — the community itself has not converged on a shared convention, which is precisely the kind of gap a revival project can usefully close.

**Decision.** For this project, the glottal stop is written with the **apostrophe (')**. Rationale:

1. **Continuity with the existing documentary record.** It matches the working notation already used in the vocabulary data and in Quesada Pacheco's own descriptive (non-chart) practice, so adopting it doesn't create a new split between "project spelling" and "source spelling" — it resolves an existing one in favor of the more heavily attested option.
2. **Keyboard and platform portability.** The apostrophe is a standard ASCII character available on every keyboard and rendering correctly across phones, social platforms, and plain text, unlike the caret-based or accent-stacking workarounds visible in the community posts above, which likely arose precisely because contributors didn't have easy access to a dedicated glottal-stop character.
3. **Intuitive iconicity for a Spanish-literate audience.** A stop/break mark reads naturally as a stop, which is a small but real advantage for learners approaching Boruca through Spanish literacy.
4. **It reduces, rather than adds to, the fragmentation** already visible both across the academic sources (\<v\> vs. apostrophe) and across community usage (caret vs. quotation mark vs. other diacritics) — see also §8, point 10, on cross-source terminological inconsistency generally.

**Open item.** It is not yet confirmed whether the caret (^) seen in the Facebook examples is being used by those writers for the *same* sound as the apostrophe in the academic sources, or for something else (e.g., nasalization or a tone-related mark) — some practical Chibchan-family orthographies do use caret-like marks for nasal vowels rather than glottal stop. Before publishing learner-facing materials that silently "correct" community spellings to the apostrophe, it would be worth confirming this with a fluent reader or with the maintainers of the *muntabrunkajctegat* / Patrimonio Brunka pages, both to avoid conflating two distinct sounds and as a matter of respectful engagement with the community already doing this work.

**Historical footnote.** The apostrophe's prevalence in the corpus is not simply an academic inconsistency — it has a documented history. Quesada Pacheco (2011) notes that Maroto Rojas's original practical alphabet (the one underlying the earliest manuscript materials) was used "de manera inconsistente" ('inconsistently') before Constenla & Maroto's 1979 regularized version fixed \<v\> as the official glottal-stop symbol. That means the apostrophe forms found in Maroto's own manuscripts, in the 1997–98 adjective paper (which draws on those manuscripts), and in the vocabulary spreadsheet are best understood as a *pre-standardization* convention that simply never fully died out in practice — not a stray typo. This project's adoption of the apostrophe can be read as continuing that older working practice rather than inventing a new one.

**Primary-source confirmation (2026).** Maroto Rojas's own 1999 posthumous critical edition (*Lengua o dialecto [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474) o brúnkajk*, ed. Quesada Pacheco) shows exactly what that pre-standardization mark looked like, in Maroto's own words: on p. 29 he proposes a diacritic — printed as ˅, transcribed in this project's source files as a superscript ᵛ — for what he calls a word's "fuercita fuerte" ('little strong force'), giving minimal-pair examples like *"Agua" [se](dictionary.html?q=se) pronuncia Dĭᵛ y no dí* ('"Water" is pronounced Dĭᵛ, not dí'). This predates Constenla & Maroto's 1979 official alphabet by several years and appears to be an independent, self-taught attempt at the same problem — worth noting because its shape (a caret-like mark) is suggestively similar to the unrelated-looking caret (^) that current community members use informally on social media (see the "Open item" above). Whether that's a genuine unbroken notational lineage or pure coincidence of two people reaching for the same available symbol is not established, but it's a more concrete historical thread than was available when the "Open item" question was first raised, and worth investigating before assuming the community caret usage is unconnected to Maroto's own orthographic history. See `boruca_maroto_rojas_synthesis.md` §5 for the full source.

## 3. Nominal Morphology: Adjectives

The fullest treatment of adjective morphology is Quesada Pacheco's "El adjetivo boruca" (1997–98), which builds on and revises earlier work by Pittier (1896/1941), Arroyo (1966), Constenla (in Constenla & Maroto 1979), and especially Rojas (1992). Quesada Pacheco identifies **seven productive adjectival suffixes**, each with characteristic semantic domains, plus notes on a fading grade/intensity system.

### 3.1 The suffix -uá (nominalizing / instrumental, "perfective" reading)
Forms nouns denoting the instrument used to perform an action; found abundantly in coinages for modern objects:
- *[huic](dictionary.html?q=huic)* 'soul' + *chij* 'to capture' → *[huic](dictionary.html?q=huic) chijguá* 'camera'
- *seján* 'cold' + *sodíj* 'to make' → *seján sádijuá* 'refrigerator'
- *[di'](dictionary.html?q=di%27)* 'water' + *broj* 'to boil' → *[di'](dictionary.html?q=di%27) brojguá* 'coffee-maker'

A secondary, reduced function is the gerund: *cun̈uá* 'flying'.

### 3.2 The suffix -shi (positional/stative adjectives)
Marks adjectives of position, state, or situation: *[anté'shi](dictionary.html#e-2083)* 'leaning, reclined', *becshi* 'mounted, straddling', *bi'shi* 'buried', *cáchi'shi* 'covered', *crástacshi* 'legs spread', *shuajshi* 'face-down'. Some bases accept both *-shi* and *-uá*, with a systematic contrast: *-shi* yields an adjective denoting position, *-uá* yields a noun denoting the instrument of the action, e.g. *anté'* 'to lean' → *[anté'shi](dictionary.html#e-2083)* 'leaned' (adj.) vs. *anté'uá* 'quilt' (n.); *[cap](dictionary.html?q=cap)* 'to sleep' → *capshi* 'asleep' vs. *[cabuá](dictionary.html?q=cabu%C3%A1)* 'bedroom'; *úshe* 'to dress' → *ushéshi* 'dressed' vs. *úsheuá* 'garment'. The suffix has phonetically conditioned allomorphs: *-sí* after dentals (*tutsi'* 'stretched') and *-í* after the velar nasal (*[cuiní](dictionary.html?q=cuin%C3%AD)* 'placed', from *cuín* 'to put'; *yacaní* 'led by the hand').

### 3.3 The suffix -rani (attenuative degree, contested)
Rojas (1992) analyzed *-rani* as marking an attenuated ("somewhat X") degree; Quesada Pacheco's data from Maroto's manuscripts confirm this general pattern (*but* 'to sweeten' → *budrani* 'half-sweet'; *[tura'](dictionary.html#e-4335)* 'to rot' → *tura'rani* 'somewhat rotten'; *rut* 'to burn' → *rudrani* 'somewhat burnt') but also found some informants confusing the attenuative with the intensive grade (§3.4) — evidence, in the author's view, that the language's grade system was already breaking down among the last speakers. *-rani* is noted as considerably more productive than the intensive suffix that follows.

### 3.4 The suffix -ón/ (intensive degree)
Marks an intensified ("very X") reading, but — unlike *-rani* — restricted to specific semantic classes: states of matter (*bal'gón/* 'very bitter', *turavón/* 'very rotten'), textures (*ajgán/* 'very strong/tough', *crasán/* 'very resistant'), temperatures (*ro'gón/* 'very hot/burning', *sejón/* 'very cold'), and mental/emotional states (*shugón/* 'very nervous', *yrojón/* 'very tired'). Adjectives outside these classes cannot take *-ón/*; intensity for them is instead expressed periphrastically with the adverb *cuín/* 'very'. The manuscripts of Espíritu Santo Maroto even show *-ón/*-marked adjectives reinforced redundantly with *cuín/* (e.g. *cuín/crasón/* 'very resistant'), which Quesada Pacheco interprets as a sign of ongoing desemanticization/loss of this paradigm.

### 3.5 The suffix -át (the most productive; "inherent quality")
By far the most common and semantically broadest adjectival suffix, marking inherent qualities across several domains: colors (*[drubát](dictionary.html#e-3181)* 'red', *[hua'gát](dictionary.html#e-3297)* 'green', *yiát* 'yellow'), shapes (*[basát](dictionary.html?q=bas%C3%A1t)* 'round', *[cajbát](dictionary.html?q=cajb%C3%A1t)* 'horizontal'), states of matter (*canát* 'hard', *[cáxenát](dictionary.html#e-2568)* 'raw', *chevát* 'sick'), weather/atmospheric phenomena (*[ba'gát](dictionary.html#e-2153)* 'clear/dawn-related', *[seját](dictionary.html#e-3784)* 'cold', *[tebejdát](dictionary.html?q=tebejd%C3%A1t)* 'moon-related'), positions (*[beát](dictionary.html?q=be%C3%A1t)* 'hidden', *[ijxiát](dictionary.html#e-3365)* 'crouched'), ages (*[bojgát](dictionary.html?q=bojg%C3%A1t)* 'young [male]', *[bushát](dictionary.html?q=bush%C3%A1t)* 'young [female]', *[ramát](dictionary.html#e-3602)* 'female', *xidát* 'newborn'), and dimensions (*[biogát](dictionary.html?q=biog%C3%A1t)* 'long', *[manát](dictionary.html#e-3422)* 'wide', *euját* 'short'). Pittier (1896/1941) already noted this suffix's productivity, though without discussing the rest of the system.

### 3.6 The suffix -é (compositional adjectives)
Applies to nominal or verbal roots to form adjectives denoting the base's implicit quality (*ram* 'female' → *[ramé](dictionary.html#e-3603)* 'female [adj.]'; *[drup](dictionary.html#e-808)* 'to blaze' → *[drubé](dictionary.html#e-3180)* 'brilliant'). Distinctively, many *-é* adjectives are compounds of two roots whose combined meaning conveys iterativity/habituality: *[xu'](dictionary.html#e-1855)* 'to flee' + *dibin/* → *xu'é dibin/* 'skittish, wild'; *[tec](dictionary.html?q=tec)* 'to speak' + *yén/* 'to show' → *tegué yén/* 'chatty, talkative'.

### 3.7 The suffix -i'shi (negative present participle)
A low-productivity suffix forming negated participial adjectives: *bag* 'to have' → *bagui'shi* 'lacking, without'; *[tec](dictionary.html?q=tec)* 'to speak' → *[teguí'shi](dictionary.html#e-4253)* 'non-speaking, mute'.

### 3.8 Semantic hierarchy proposed by Quesada Pacheco
The paper concludes with a hierarchical semantic schema organizing the adjectival system around binary features:
- **[+ inherent quality]** → *-át*
- **[– inherent quality]** splits into:
  - **[+ resultative]** → **[+ perfective]** *-uá* vs. **[– perfective]** *-shi*
  - **[– resultative]** → **[+ intensity]** *-ón/* vs. **[– intensity]**, further split by **[+ process]** *-rani* vs. **[– process]** *-cá* (a "state" suffix noted in passing but not treated in depth in this paper)

This schema explains apparent near-synonyms that Spanish translation collapses but Boruca formally distinguishes, e.g. *ejtcá* '(is currently) thin' vs. *[ejdát](dictionary.html#e-3238)* '(is inherently) thin' vs. *ejduá* 'thinned/made thin'; *cutcá* '(is) full' vs. *[cudát](dictionary.html#e-2825)* 'pot-bellied'. The author stresses this schema is provisional and that further study is needed, particularly around the productivity of *-uá* with transitive verb bases (possibly linked to transitivity marking generally, an area the paper flags as unstudied) and the fact that *-cá*-final adjectives freely co-occur with *-rani*, *-ón/*, and *-át* but never with *-shi*.

## 4. Determiners/Clitics: the *ki* Clitic

The clitic **ki** is one of the most distinctive and grammatically productive elements in Boruca, and is treated in depth by Quesada & Quesada Pacheco (1995, "Boruca *ki*: de definido a específico y la hipótesis de las funciones contiguas"). *Ki*'s behavior is unusual among Chibchan languages generally, since the word class "article" is not common in the family — Boruca appears to be innovating one from scratch, which the authors take as one of several signs (along with the infinitive marker, see §5.1) that Boruca may be typologically distinctive within Chibchan.

### 4.1 Proposed etymology
The authors propose that *ki* arose through resegmentation of sequences ending in root-final **-k** plus the third-person/demonstrative marker **i** (< proto-Chibchan *hi 'thing, that'). This *i* independently gives rise, depending on whether it attaches to a verbal or nominal base, to (a) the free third-person pronoun *í* and a "lemmatic" citation-form proclitic, when attached to verbs, and (b) a nominalizer and — via reanalysis — the article *ki*, when attached to nouns. Proposed reanalyses include: *kapsúxk-i* > *kapsux-ki* ('this sleepy-one' → 'the sleepy one'); *so'tóxk-i* > *sotróx-ki* ('this old-one' → 'the old lady'); *brúnkaxk-i* > *brúnkax-ki* ('this Boruca-one' → 'the [Boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474) [person]'). The same *-k* loss process [is](dictionary.html?q=is) independently evidenced by the verb *ték* 'to speak' losing its final /k/ before the past-tense marker *-krá* (→ *tegrá*), which the authors take as reinforcing evidence for the resegmentation hypothesis.

### 4.2 The Function Contiguity Hypothesis and the grammaticalization path
Drawing on Blansitt's (1988) "function contiguity hypothesis" (originally developed for allative/dative markers), the authors argue that *ki*'s evolving functions trace a single continuum of increasing **topicality/specificity**, moving through the following stages, each attested with textual examples:

1. **Definite article** on simple nouns in subject, object, and oblique position: *tebek ki* 'the snake', *tebek kí kawí'ta brúnkax tá* 'The snake lives in Boruca.'
2. **Extension to demonstrative-headed NPs**: *ki* cliticizes to the more grammaticalized demonstratives *wá* and *yá* (e.g. *wá ki* 'this one'), but not to the less grammaticalized *wé'* and *tí'* (later developments that arose after *ki* itself).
3. **Extension to noun+adjective phrases and relative clauses**, where *ki* attaches to the whole phrase, not just the head noun: *kútni moréng ki* 'the good pig'; *[ú](dictionary.html#e-4380) sodíxkrin̈ ki morénra* 'the house that they built is good.'
4. **Extension to proper names**: *Kwasráng ki é'tse kóntox(ki) ái'kra* 'Kuasrán killed a man' — a step attested cross-linguistically (cf. Sp. *la María*, It. *il Carlo*).
5. **Grammaticalization as subject marker on personal pronouns**, now nearly obligatory: *[át](dictionary.html?q=%C3%A1t) ki* (1SG), *[bá](dictionary.html?q=b%C3%A1) ki* (2SG), *[í](dictionary.html?q=%C3%AD) ki* (3SG), and their plural counterparts *dit róxk ki*, *[bi'](dictionary.html?q=bi%27) tóxk ki*, *i róxk ki*. Notably *ki* does **not** appear when these same referents are objects or obliques, nor when the emphatic/focus marker *ang* is present (*[át](dictionary.html?q=%C3%A1t) ki báxtkrin* 'he hit me' is ungrammatical with *ki* on the pronominal object).
6. **Optional/discourse-conditioned marking of oblique arguments**, correlating with benefactive/malefactive role and discourse prominence — the same referent (e.g. the culture-hero Cuasrán) can appear with or without *ki* depending on its relative topicality in context, a pattern the authors compare to differential object marking with Spanish *a*.
7. **Combination with the indefinite article é'tse.** *Ki* co-occurs with the indefinite marker *é'tse* when the indefinite referent is nonetheless discursively specified/emphasized (e.g. with evaluative adjectives like *brát* 'big', *kri'é* 'beautiful', *sungróx* 'old', *búj*, *[só'](dictionary.html#e-3847)*). It is excluded when *é'tse* denotes an age ('one year'), when *relf* 'only' or *dó'l* 'like' are present, in adjective sequences, in possessor+possessed constructions, before objects of postpositions, in indefinite copular complements, or when the emphasizers *[abí](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD)*/*ang* are present. This distributional split shows *ki*'s function here is specifically to mark **specificity**, not (in)definiteness per se — capturing the ambiguity of a phrase like Sp. "me caso con una francesa" (a French woman I know vs. one I don't).
8. **Specificity marking in temporal expressions**: *duxbi'k ki tá* 'last night', *tsáx ki tá* 'today', *é'tse dék ki tá* 'once (upon a time)'.
9. **Further grammaticalization as a subordinator**, introducing complement clauses with verbs of saying/thinking (*verba cogitandi et dicendi*): *[át](dictionary.html?q=%C3%A1t) ki tekrá ki...* 'I said that...'; *ógé bin ték dóxkta ki...* 'You have all heard that...'. This use is plausibly a Spanish-influenced calque of *[que](dictionary.html?q=que)* but is argued to be independently supported by *ki*'s prior grammaticalization as a specificity marker, paralleling how demonstratives become subordinators in Germanic languages (cf. Eng. *that*, Ger. *dass* < demonstrative *das*).

The authors summarize the overall path as: **REANALYSIS > DEFINITENESS > SPECIFICATION**, with the last stage branching into three further specializations — (a) marking indefinite-but-specific referents, (b) marking pronominal subjects, and (c) functioning as a subordinator — each requiring that *ki* have already reached the "specifier" stage, which the authors present as evidence for the unidirectionality principle of grammaticalization theory.

### 4.3 A formal grammaticalization model, and independent 19th-century historical evidence (2026 addition)

Two further sources, newly folded into this project, substantially deepen §4.1–4.2: J. Diego Quesada's own follow-up study, "The Grammaticalization of SPECIFICITY (and Beyond) in Boruca" (*IJAL* 66(3), 2000), and Quesada Pacheco's "La lengua boruca en manuscritos del siglo XIX" (*Estudios de Lingüística Chibcha* 13, 1994), which gives *ki*'s behavior a documented anchor point roughly 130 years earlier than any other source in this project.

**The 2000 formal model.** Quesada (2000) recasts the same data as a three-stage grammaticalization chain — **DEFINITE ARTICLE (Greenberg's Stage I) > SPECIFICITY MARKER (Stage II) > SUBJECT MARKER (Stage III)** — and, crucially, explains *why* obligatory marking landed specifically on subject pronouns rather than objects or obliques: Boruca personal pronouns are formally syncretic between subject and object function (the same pronoun form serves both roles), so *ki* was recruited specifically to disambiguate subject from object anaphora — "the morphologization of subject in this language" (Quesada, in progress, cited therein). This reframes §4.2 point 5 above: the near-obligatory *ki* on subject pronouns is not really about specificity anymore by the time it reaches that stage — it has been reanalyzed as pure subject-marking, even though it arose historically through the specificity channel. Quesada gives two distributional tables cross-tabulating *ki*-marking by grammatical relation (subject/direct object/oblique) and by semantic case role (agent/patient/benefactive/locative-instrumental) against three NP types (proper nouns, demonstrative NPs, pronouns): subjects require *ki* categorically across all three NP types; direct objects and obliques allow it only optionally, and only for full NPs (proper nouns, demonstratives) — pronominal objects/obliques with *ki* are ungrammatical outright (**\*at ki bahdring** 'he hits me' [is](dictionary.html?q=is) out, confirming the ungrammaticality already noted in §4.2 point 5). A comparative-Chibchan note worth carrying forward: Quesada states plainly that no other Chibchan language has grammaticalized a specificity marker along this path, making *ki* a genuine within-family innovation, not just an areal quirk. This paper is one of the two "bibliography leads" flagged in `boruca_three_sources_synthesis.md` §[Castro reading notes]; it directly and substantially answers Gap 7 (subject-marking) below, alongside Castro's independent nominative-accusative argument (§6.1).

**19th-century historical evidence.** Quesada Pacheco (1994) reanalyzes *[qui](dictionary.html?q=qui)* (the 19th-century spelling of *ki*) as it appears across the four 19th-century manuscript sources (Valentini 1862, Gabb 1875, Thiel 1882, Pittier 1892–96/1941) and finds the same basic distribution already in place — but with one clear diachronic difference. As in the modern language, *[qui](dictionary.html?q=qui)* already marked subject pronouns categorically (*At [qui](dictionary.html?q=qui) [ba](dictionary.html?q=ba) tzai yúra* 'I help you', Thiel) and attached to common nouns much as a definite article does (*Kuinge sai-ra viaje [qui](dictionary.html?q=qui) [ta](dictionary.html?q=ta)* 'we sweated a lot on the trip'). With proper names, however, 19th-century *[qui](dictionary.html?q=qui)* was still **facultative** rather than obligatory — the sources show the same name attested both with and without it (*Ponciano deg-dja-ra* / *José aa dé-bin-ra...* both lack it; *Kuasrán [qui](dictionary.html?q=qui)...* type constructions occur but are not consistent) — where the modern language, per §4.2 point 4, treats *ki*-marking of proper names as essentially required. This is a genuine, citable diachronic data point: it suggests the extension of *ki* to proper nouns was still mid-grammaticalization as late as the 1890s and has since become more categorical, consistent with (and independently corroborating) the unidirectionality claim central to both the 1995 and 2000 papers. The 19th-century sources also already show *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* (spelled *an*, *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* — the "destematizer," per one analysis; see §4.4 for a fuller and more current account, including why that label is now contested) in productive use, including fused forms with pronouns matching the modern paradigm (*at + an = [añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)*, *[ba](dictionary.html?q=ba) + an = [bañ](dictionary.html#e-2135)*, etc.), and one directly attested case of *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* combining with the imperative — *Ding [ba](dictionary.html?q=ba) txi uringa!* 'careful, don't forget' — which Quesada Pacheco notes does not otherwise co-occur with the negative imperative marker in the modern language (Constenla 1979:48), a small but genuine morphosyntactic wrinkle not otherwise documented in this project's corpus.

### 4.4 What *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)*/*ang* actually is: a contested analysis, and the case for "focus/topic marker" over "destematizer" (2026 addition)

§4.3 above (following Quesada Pacheco 1994) and §6.1/§7.3 below (following Castro) both refer to essentially the same morpheme — variously spelled *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* or *ang*, and glossed variously as a "destematizer" or a "focus marker" — without reconciling the two labels. J. Diego Quesada's dedicated study, "Enfoque y topicalidad en boruca: el caso de *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)*" (*Estudios de Lingüística Chibcha* 15: 43-58, 1996; full synthesis in `boruca_quesada_1996_anj_synthesis.md`), is the source that actually adjudicates this, and its conclusion is that "destematizer" is the wrong label.

**The fused-pronoun morphology itself is not in dispute.** Every source agrees on the portmanteau paradigm already given in §4.3: *at+añ=añ*, *bá+añ=bañ*, *i+añ=iñ*, plural *diʔ+añ=diñ*, *biʔ+añ=biñ*, *i rójc+añ=iñ [rójc](dictionary.html?q=r%C3%B3jc)* — and this is the same morpheme Castro's papers romanize *ang* and describe as restricted to subject function (§6.1) and as fusing onto finite verbs (e.g. *So' Juana ang ohrí' sodíh-ra* 'Doña Juana makes tortillas', §7.3) — an example that is, tellingly, word-for-word the same sentence J.D. Quesada (1996) uses to illustrate *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)*'s free-form focus function. **Castro's "focus marker ang" and Quesada Pacheco's "destematizer añ" are the same element**, described independently by two different research lineages using two different labels.

**The dispute is about what the morpheme *does*, not what it *[is](dictionary.html?q=is)*.** The "Hipótesis de Destematización" (HD) — Constenla & Maroto (1979, 1986) and Rojas (1990) — holds that *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* marks a subject's *loss* of topicality/prominence, typically when displaced from clause-initial position. J.D. Quesada (1996) argues this is backwards, on both theoretical and empirical grounds: (a) it requires a language to overtly mark the *unmarked*/default case, which is typologically unusual (languages generally mark the *marked* category); (b) not every "detopicalized" subject actually takes *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* under HD's own data, an inconsistency HD cannot explain; (c) *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* is required in contexts — focused wh-subject questions (*dí [añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1) [i'](dictionary.html#e-3409) áivcra* '¿quién FOC lo mató?'), purpose clauses, hortatives, and negative imperatives — that have nothing to do with word-order displacement. His alternative: *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* is fundamentally a **subject-focus marker**, whose free (unfused) form marks contrastive/emphatic focus on subjects, and which then **grammaticalizes along a well-attested cross-linguistic path** — FOCUS > SUBJECT RELATIVIZER > ANAPHORIC/TOPIC-CONTINUITY CONCORD — with the fused/cliticized forms (*[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1), [bañ](dictionary.html#e-2135), [iñ](dictionary.html#e-3373)...*) representing the most advanced, most eroded stage of that chain, now functioning to track a *highly persistent, continuing* topic across clauses (the mirror image of "destematization"). A speculative fourth, earlier stage is also floated: that *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* may once have been a genuine topic-prominent-language topic marker (paralleling Tagalog, Cebuano, Japanese *wa*), a possibility left open for future research.

**A genuinely new function this source adds**: *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* also serves as a **subject relativizer** — introducing relative clauses only when the relativized head is itself the subject of the relative clause (*duríj [rójc](dictionary.html?q=r%C3%B3jc) [añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1) [cháj](dictionary.html#e-2971) [cácba'](dictionary.html#e-2425) cahuí'-ra [qui](dictionary.html?q=qui) [rá'](dictionary.html?q=r%C3%A1%27) rójc-ra* 'the shamans who live today are Talamancans') — a function not previously documented anywhere else in this project's corpus, and one that fits neatly as an intermediate stage in the grammaticalization chain between the focus and anaphoric-concord functions.

**Recommendation**: this grammar should present *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)*/*ang* as **contested terminology for one morpheme**, not as two different things — the reference-grammar and revival-curriculum default should be Quesada's (1996) "focus/topic-continuity" account, both because it is the more recent, more argued-for analysis (with an explicit rebuttal of HD's specific empirical problems) and because it is the account already implicitly adopted by Castro's independent "focus marker ang" terminology in §6.1/§7.3 — "destematizer" should be retained only as a historical label for the older (1979/1986/1990) analysis, not used as if it were the settled term. This also meaningfully deepens Gap 7 below: Quesada (1996), together with Castro (2008) and Quesada (2000), means all three of the *ki*/*[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)*/*[abí](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD)* subject-marking cluster now have dedicated focused studies, though (per Quesada 1996 note 7) at least one real counterexample — a focus-fronted postpositional phrase that does *not* trigger *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* — remains unexplained even in the most recent account.

## 5. The Verbal System

The most extensive treatment of Boruca verbal morphology is Castro's "The Boruca Verbal Dimension" (Amerindia 2012), supplemented by Quesada Pacheco's "La causatividad en el boruca" (2011) for causative constructions and Abarca González's frequency study for usage patterns in narrative.

### 5.1 Verb structure and infinitives

Boruca verbs are not divisible into morphological classes (unlike Spanish's -ar/-er/-ir classes, or Teribe's static/dynamic classes); Boruca verbs are recognizable only on semantic (state/process/action) and syntactic (VP head, sentence-final position) grounds. Sample prototypical verbs by class: **state** — *tsá'* 'be cold', *jióng* 'be hot', *jri'ká* 'be alive', *kohtká* 'be dead'; **process** — *koht* 'die', *[tura'](dictionary.html#e-4335)* 'rot', *uh* 'melt', *kwik* 'dance', *tabájeng* 'sing', *tek* 'speak', *kap* 'sleep'; **action** — *[ái'](dictionary.html?q=%C3%A1i%27)* 'kill', *báht* 'hit', *[shité'](dictionary.html#e-4051)* 'break'.

Distinctively among Chibchan languages, Boruca has a dedicated **infinitive marker, -í (variant -íh)** — a feature otherwise absent from the family and, together with *ki* (§4), one of the features Castro cites as suggesting Boruca may occupy a separate branch of Chibchan. The infinitive appears immediately after a governing matrix verb or within a subordinated postpositional phrase, e.g. *róhk shon-ra já' shon-kra róhk teg-íh* 'they gather to talk' (talk-**INF**). It is deleted when TAM suffixes attach directly to the root, which is offered as morphological evidence of its inflectional (not derivational) status. Curiously, its distribution is **intermittent**: in near-identical syntactic contexts it sometimes appears and sometimes does not, with no consistency found in the data; Castro speculates this may be phonologically conditioned but leaves this for future study. The infinitive is *not* the citation form — the bare (uninflected) root is used for that — which raises the possibility that the infinitive marker is itself a kind of inflection rather than a true non-finite citation form.

**Full verb-structure template** (Castro 2012): **[ROOT (INF) (AKT) ~ (MOD) (NEG) (ASP) (SUBJ-IS) (MOOD)]**. Concretely this yields possibilities such as: bare root (*[tec](dictionary.html?q=tec)* 'to speak/talk'); root+infinitive (*teg-íh* 'to talk'); root+Aktionsart+negation+aspect (*ishd-áh-r-í-ng* 'he just saw'); root+modality+negation+aspect (*juak-ch-í'sh-irá* 'did not want to say'); root+mood (*[ba](dictionary.html?q=ba) dabag-á* 'you come!').

Boruca verbs classify by **valence**: intransitive (single argument; SV or VS order, e.g. *mang i-ng de-krá róhk i déng ka* 'then they went behind him'), transitive (two arguments; SOV/OVS, e.g. *e'tsi ramróhk ki é'tsi kóngróhk ki bejáng-kra* 'a woman loved a man'), and ditransitive, which require an obligatory postposition to express the third argument (*a-ng [ba](dictionary.html?q=ba) jét báhkang [só't](dictionary.html#e-3850) ki wí'-kra* 'I brought you the four bluegill'). Boruca has no true zero-valence/impersonal verbs: weather predicates use *kák* ('day/sun/climate') as a grammatical subject, and unspecified subjects are expressed with the generic *[abí](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD) róhk* ('people') or the reflexive *já'* — Castro notes Boruca patterns with Germanic languages here in disallowing null subjects. There is no free-standing copula; instead the TAM suffixes (*-ra, -íra, -kra, -kirá*) attach directly to nominal, adjectival, locative, or adverbial predicates to yield copula-like nominal/adjectival/locative predications (e.g. *[bá](dictionary.html?q=b%C3%A1) ki á'd be-rá* 'you are my mother'; *ógé kahkshí-ra já ki kangkwá's ki ang* 'that stone is all open').

### 5.2 Tense, Aspect, and Mood — the case against a strict tense analysis

This is the centerpiece of Castro's verbal-dimension paper, and it is directly reinforced (using largely the same argument and data) by Castro, Murillo & Quesada's comparative paper "Evolución morfosintáctica en las lenguas chibchas" (2009).

**The traditional "Tense Hypothesis."** Constenla & Maroto (1986) and Quesada Pacheco (1995, 1996) analyzed the core TAM markers **-ra, -kra, -irá, -kirá** as a tense system: *-ra* = non-past (present/future); *-kra* = perfective past; *-irá* = imperfective past; *-kirá* = pluperfect (a "relative" past). They additionally posited a non-obligatory "second series" of pre-tense markers: *-ah* 'already', *-krog* 'finish', *-cha* 'want', *-mang* 'used to', *-ishkong* 'have to'.

**Castro's critique and reanalysis.** Castro argues this hypothesis (i) is asymmetric — it posits a pluperfect with no corresponding perfect; (ii) is not purely temporal, since the *-kra/-irá* opposition is itself aspectual (perfective/imperfective), a familiar cross-linguistic pattern; and (iii) fails empirically — in narrative texts, *-ra*-marked verbs regularly occur where a present-tense reading is impossible and only an "actual"/ongoing-narration reading works, and vice versa. She reanalyzes the same four markers as a purely **aspectual system**:

| | Imperfective | | Perfective | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | **Actual** (-ra) | **Durative** (-irá) | **Punctual** (-kra) | **Anterior** (-kirá) |

In this view *-ra* is a narrative/actual marker (not "present tense"), and the system's internal-constituency-of-situation logic — rather than temporal sequencing — governs distribution. Perfective (*-kra*) verbs foreground main narrative events; imperfective (*-irá*) verbs mark descriptive/background material, following Binnick's (1991) foreground/background distinction.

**The special status of -kirá.** Castro further shows that *-kirá*, glossed by earlier authors as "pluperfect," better patterns as a pure **anteriority** marker not requiring a syntactic point of reference — supported by three independent facts: (1) its frequency is very low relative to the other three markers even in the earliest (1892–96) Pittier texts; (2) the semi-speaker Eligia Maroto reportedly did not recognize or use the form at all (Quesada Pacheco, p.c. 2008); (3) even in the 19th-century Pittier corpus, *-kirá* tokens are left unglossed (unlike the rest of the text), suggesting difficulty assigning it meaning even then. Castro's conclusion — corroborated in the 2009 comparative paper — is that **Boruca was in transition from a tense language to a tenseless (purely aspectual) language, a shift already underway by the late 19th century** and evidently incomplete at the point of the language's extinction.

**Aktionsart.** Castro reanalyzes Constenla & Maroto's "second series" not as a distinct verb series but as **Aktionsart** (manner-of-action) derivational affixes, distinct in kind from the aspectual (TAM) markers and preceding them in the verb template. These are organized into phases plus two other categories:
- **Imminential** (*ja*, free morpheme) 'about to X': *ja-rá ba-ng tru'-í* 'you are about to fall'
- **Ingressive** (*já sa'*) 'to start'
- **Continuative** (*do'á*) 'to continue': *do'á dabag-ra...* 'continue to arrive...'
- **Terminative** (*-krog*) 'to finish/stop': *i'íng-krog-írá* 'they finished tying it'
- **Egressive** (*-áh*) 'to have just X, already': *i'ishd-áh-r-í-ng* 'they just saw it'
- **Consuetudinary** (*-mang*) 'used to': *jaí-mang-kra* 'were used to going'
- **Intensity** (*[bra'é](dictionary.html#e-154)*, free morpheme) 'excessively': *[í](dictionary.html?q=%C3%AD) ki tegu-i bra'é-ra* 'he talks excessively'

Notably, Castro traces a plausible **diachronic path from causativity to egressive Aktionsart**: the fossilized causative collocation *chikáh* ('to make [a] sound', < *chik* 'sound' + *ah* 'make') suggests *-ah* originally had causative force, which bled into the now much more frequent egressive use of *-áh*.

**Mood and Modality.** Imperative is marked by *-a* (*[ba](dictionary.html?q=ba) tú'a* 'eat!') or, alternatively, by the ordinary "actual" aspect marker *-ra*; imperatives cannot take the regular negator *-i'shi-*, but instead use the preverbal free particle *dí* (*dí bi-ng dabag-á* 'don't come!'). The **exhortative** *cha* occurs sentence-initially (*chá-ng a'r úhtsa ki shúng-ra...* 'come, I want to send...'). **Desiderative** modality is a bound root suffix (*i kabát ék-ír-í-ng...i juak-ch-í'sh-irá* 'she didn't want to tell him'); **deontic** modality ('have to') is expressed by *-ishkóng* (*róhk ái'-ishkóng* 'in order to kill it').

### 5.3 Valence-adjusting operations: causatives in depth

Quesada Pacheco's "La causatividad en el boruca" (2011) is a full monograph-length treatment situating Boruca causatives within Comrie's/Shibatani's/Seiler's typological frameworks (lexical–morphological–analytic continuum; direct vs. indirect causation; control hierarchies). Empirically, Boruca shows **three types of causative construction** plus a **fourth, apparently unique mechanism**:

**(a) Lexical causatives.** No morphological marking distinguishes causative from non-causative use; only context/argument structure disambiguates: *Juana cabrá* 'Juana sleeps' vs. *Juana [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) iv huáv [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) cabrá* 'Juana puts her child to sleep' (lit. 'Juana [makes] her child sleep'); *úvshi [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) dabavcrá* 'the jar broke' vs. *Pedro [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) úvshi [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) dabavcrá* 'Pedro broke the jar.'

**(b) Morphological causatives (non-productive).** Only three verbs were found bearing a clear causative suffix **-óv**: *[yo](dictionary.html#e-2005)* 'to rain' → *yóv* 'to bathe'; *[dec](dictionary.html?q=dec)* 'to go' → *degóv* 'to scare off'; *ajc* 'to be strong' → *ajgóv* 'to hold on'.

**(c) Analytic/periphrastic causatives (the most productive type today).** An auxiliary verb carries the causative meaning. Two auxiliaries express **true causation**: *sodíj* 'to make/do' and *ón̈* 'to make, cause' (apparently in free variation), plus *shún̈* 'to send, cause' — which the author argues differs subtly from *ón̈* in that the causer with *shún̈* is not directly/solely responsible for the caused effect, whereas *ón̈* implies full causer responsibility. Two further forms express **permission**: *[ajt](dictionary.html?q=ajt)* 'to let' (the more common; causing event expressed in the infinitive, e.g. *María [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) iv huáv róhk [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) ajdrá cuijí* 'María lets her children play') and *evshí* 'to let' (an invariant morpheme restricted to imperative/exhortative contexts, e.g. *evshín dabagrá* 'let him come').

**(d) Suprasegmental causatives — a typologically distinctive finding.** Quesada Pacheco reports that certain monosyllabic low-tone intransitive verb roots become transitive/causative when the root shifts to **high tone**, with no other morphological change: *ucarás [qui](dictionary.html?q=qui) cajcrá* 'the door opened' vs. *María [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) [úcarás](dictionary.html#e-4383) [qui](dictionary.html?q=qui) cájccra* 'María opened the door'; *[auj](dictionary.html?q=auj) [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) rajdrá* 'the dog goes out' vs. *huáv [qui](dictionary.html?q=qui) [auj](dictionary.html?q=auj) [quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD) rájdra* 'the child lets the dog out'; *[các](dictionary.html?q=c%C3%A1c) [qui](dictionary.html?q=qui) bejtcrá* 'the sun went down' vs. *[chúp](dictionary.html#e-3049) [qui](dictionary.html?q=qui) béjtcr-n̈ róhk* 'they lowered the nest.' The author explicitly states this mechanism appears to be **unique to Boruca among the Costa Rican Chibchan languages**, even though several of them (e.g. Bribri) also have tonal systems, and treats it as a lexicalized (non-fully-productive) pattern restricted to certain monosyllabic verbs, since some informants rejected extending it to new verbs (*\*Juana qui huáv qui cábra* 'Juana puts the child to sleep' was rejected).

**Anticausatives.** A distinct suffix, **-rév** (allomorph **-dév** after consonants other than vowels/h), derives anticausative/inchoative verbs from nominal or adjectival bases, restricted semantically to natural-phenomena/biological-process verbs: *sej* 'cold' → *sejrév* 'to become cold' (vs. causative *[séj](dictionary.html#e-3783)* 'to cool [something]'); *yi* 'fire' → *yirév* 'to heat up' (vs. *yí* 'to heat'); *[sujc](dictionary.html?q=sujc)* 'old' → *sujdév* 'to age.' Unlike many cross-linguistic anticausative morphemes, *-rév* is described as unambiguous (not conflated with reflexive or passive uses).

**Productivity assessment.** The author's overall conclusion, based on elicitation from remaining speakers, is that **analytic causatives are now the preferred/most productive strategy**, while the suprasegmental (tonal) causative is unproductive/relic, situating Boruca on the following continuum: Analytic (least direct) → Suprasegmental → Morphological → Lexical (most direct causation). Notably, the paper also reports that Boruca shows **no evidence** of the prefixal causative morphology reconstructed for proto-Chibchan by Holt (1989) based on comparative evidence from Paya, Cuna, and Kágaba — whether Boruca's *-óv* suffix is historically related to those prefixes is left as an open question.

### 5.4 Predicate suffixes: frequency and usage in traditional narratives

Abarca González's thesis-based study directly quantifies which "núcleo predicativo" (predicate-core) suffixes actually occur in traditional Boruca narration, based on 1,516 tokens drawn from stories told by four elder narrators (Espíritu Santo Maroto, Isolina Céspedes, Ernesto González, Paulina Leiva) as recorded in Constenla & Maroto (1979/1986). **This is the most directly actionable data in the corpus for sequencing learner materials**, since it shows what a fluent narrator actually needs most.

| 7 | *-í'shicrá* | preterite indefinite negative | 26 | 1.72% |
| 8 | *-í'sha* | non-past negative | 19 | 1.25% |
| 9 | *-a* | imperative | 14 | 0.92% |
| 10 | *-quírá* | pluperfect | 1 | 0.07% |

**Key pedagogical takeaways:**
- **The three affirmative finite forms — imperfect (*-írá*), indefinite/perfective (*-crá*), and non-past (*-ra*) — together make up 83.05% of all finite predicate forms** and should clearly be prioritized in any teaching sequence, well ahead of the negative counterparts (7.21% combined) or the imperative (0.92%).
- **Finite forms overwhelmingly dominate non-finite (infinitive) forms**: 90.57% vs. 9.43%, consistent with infinitives being restricted to subordinate/fragmentary clauses.
- **The pluperfect (-quírá) is nearly absent** (a single token in the entire 1,516-form sample) — independently corroborating Castro's claim (§5.2) that this marker was already marginal/obsolescent, here from a completely different methodological angle (raw frequency counts rather than semantic argumentation).
- **"Second series" (Aktionsart) suffixes are rare overall** (34 tokens, 2.24%), with desiderative *-cha-* (17 tokens) and expectative *-aj-* (9 tokens) the most frequent; terminative *-krog-* appears only once. Interestingly, their distribution is highly narrator-dependent: Maroto uses desiderative/obligatory forms (higher "subjective" content) far more than the other three narrators, who barely use this series at all — a caution that "second series" frequency may reflect individual narrative style as much as grammatical productivity.
- **Periphrastic (auxiliary) verb forms are also rare** (63 tokens, 4.16%), dominated by the progressive periphrasis with auxiliary *dová* ('to do/make' + gerund-like form, 50.79% of periphrases) and the inchoative *yá'sá'* ('to begin', 30.16%).
- **Non-verbal predicate bases** (nominal, adjectival, postpositional-phrase, and adverbial predicates taking the same TAM suffixes — see §5.1 on the copula-like function) account for 10.95% of all predicative forms, confirming that this "adjectival/nominal predication" pattern is a normal, not marginal, part of everyday narrative Boruca.
- **The non-past (-ra) form is semantically the most flexible**, attested with habitual, historical-present, actual/ongoing, and future readings — e.g., 45.53% of its uses are "actual" (ongoing at speech time) and 27.09% are historical-present in narrative, a pattern strongly reminiscent of vivid oral-narrative present-tense use in Spanish.
- **Narrator fluency correlates with predicate density**: the narrator judged (by Constenla, p.c.) most fluent and expressive, Isolina Céspedes, produced by far the highest average number of predicate forms per narration (113, versus 66.68, 42.25, and 9.33 for the other three), and also showed markedly more use of adjectival predicate bases and historical-present non-past forms — suggesting that predicate density and non-past/historical-present usage may be reasonable proxies for narrative fluency in teaching evaluation.

## 6. Syntax

### 6.1 Subject and ergativity

The one source addressing this topic directly, Jack L. Wilson K.'s "Sobre la definición lingüística: el sujeto y el ergativo," turns out on inspection to contain **essentially no Boruca-specific data**. It is a general theoretical essay, situated within 1980s Government-and-Binding-era generative grammar, critiquing the imprecise and inconsistent use of terms like "subject," "ergative," "nominative," and "absolutive" across the typological literature (citing Chomsky, Fillmore, Dixon, Trask, Comrie, Keenan). Its illustrative language data is drawn almost entirely from **Bribri** (a Chibchan relative of Boruca, but a distinct language) contrasted with Russian, English, French, and Spanish, to argue that the ergative/accusative distinction is largely terminological rather than a deep typological split, and that Bribri's "ergative" marker *-tö* likely descends historically from an erstwhile active/passive voice opposition (following Trask's account of the passive-to-ergative diachronic pathway) rather than representing canonical ergativity. **No Boruca examples, morphology, or argument-marking data appear anywhere in this paper.** For a revival grammar, this source is useful only as background typological framing (i.e., as a caution about how "subject" and "ergative" are used loosely in the broader literature) and should not be cited as evidence about Boruca's own argument-marking system; the actual Boruca subject-marking system is best drawn instead from the *ki* clitic study (§4) and from Castro's papers, which show that Boruca subjects are marked via the clitics *ki*, *ang*, and *[abí](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD)* (grammaticalized markers of subject/topic/focus/emphasis, per Castro 2012 fn. 6) rather than by a case system in the Indo-European or ergative-absolutive sense. Boruca does not appear to be ergative in the classical sense in any of the other seven sources; rather, its argument-marking relies on word order (basic SOV, though not rigid) plus the discourse-conditioned clitic system described in §4.

**Castro's nominative-accusative argument (2008).** Where Wilson's paper is silent, Castro's "Brúnkahk Tek: An Extinct Language" (2008) actually takes up the question directly, using two-verb-clause coreference tests and coordination diagnostics — the standard cross-linguistic toolkit for distinguishing nominative-accusative from ergative-absolutive alignment — to argue that Boruca is **nominative-accusative, not ergative**. Her paper gives a concrete distributional split between the two subject-associated markers: ***ang* [is](dictionary.html?q=is) restricted to subject function** (both nominal and pronominal subjects take it), while ***ki* covers subject function** (nominal and pronominal alike) **and object function, but only for nominal objects — never pronominal ones**. In other words, *ki* is not simply "the subject marker" or "the object marker" in the way a case-marking language would have one dedicated form per grammatical relation; its distribution cuts across the subject/object divide and is instead conditioned by the nominal-vs-pronominal status of the argument, which is itself a diagnostic hallmark of accusative (not ergative) alignment. Castro additionally states plainly that "Boruca is the only Chibchan language that exhibits the presence of an article: *ki*" — framing *ki* as an article in at least some of its uses, alongside (not necessarily in place of) its function as a subject/object clitic, a terminological wrinkle worth flagging for anyone reconciling this account with the *ki*-clitic description in §4. Castro is explicit that her own treatment is preliminary — "a brief characterization... in future papers, a more thorough analysis will be made" — so this does not fully close the gap identified below (§8, gap 7), but it is real, source-attributed Boruca-specific evidence where none existed before, and should be treated as the current best answer to the alignment question pending any fuller study. (Castro's *ang* here is the same morpheme discussed at length, under the variant spelling *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)*, in the new §4.4 — see there for J.D. Quesada's 1996 dedicated study of its focus/topic-continuity function.)

**The negation-marker dispute.** A related morphological disagreement, also raised by Castro (2008), concerns how many distinct negative markers Boruca has. Quesada Pacheco's (1995) *Hablemos [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)* analysis proposes a **different negative marker for each TAM category** (i.e., negation is bound up with the tense/aspect system, one form per paradigm cell). Castro argues instead for a **single negative marker**, the suffix ***í'shi*** (with phonologically-conditioned surface variants), used uniformly across all TAM categories — and she states explicitly that Quesada Pacheco's per-TAM analysis may reflect "an erroneous analysis of the data," though she allows it might instead simply be a simplification made for pedagogical reasons in a teaching grammar rather than a genuine descriptive error. This project's corpus does not contain enough independent textual evidence to adjudicate between the two analyses, but a revival grammar or teaching curriculum should be aware that the "one negator per tense" pattern taught in *Hablemos [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)* is a **contested**, not settled, analysis, and that Castro's single-suffix account is the more recent and more argued-for alternative (see also imperatives' distinct negator *dí*, §5.2, which both analyses would treat as a separate, non-TAM-conditioned form).

### 6.2 Complex clauses and subordination

Castro's "The Complex Clause in Boruca" (2010) provides the fullest syntactic treatment in the corpus, organized around the classic **parataxis/hypotaxis** distinction (following Lehmann 1988).

**Paratactic (coordinate) constructions:**
- **Asyndetic** (juxtaposed, no connective): clauses may share no elements (*Káhwik Kwasráng ki ká o-krá [yo](dictionary.html#e-2005) [brú'](dictionary.html#e-2337) ki. Kak ki ro'-krá.* 'Far from Kwasráng, the big rain finished. The sun shined.') or share arguments (overt, or via zero anaphora) or verbs.
- **Syndetic** (connective-linked): the four principal connectives are **mang, ní, ihchí, iné** ('then', roughly 'and/that', 'and', 'and/but'). Boruca shows a strong preference for syndetic over asyndetic coordination. *[Ní](dictionary.html?q=N%C3%AD)* is polyfunctional — it can coordinate clauses (*A'd beyáng-r-i-ng, [ní](dictionary.html?q=n%C3%AD) [át](dictionary.html?q=%C3%A1t) ki [i'](dictionary.html#e-3409) beyáng-ra i do'sh* 'He loves me and I love him too') or subordinate a complement clause (*Ramróhk búsh ki kraé'kra ni [i'](dictionary.html#e-3409) wá' ki baw'íra* 'The woman observed that her own child was being born'), with function disambiguated only by context. *[Iné](dictionary.html#e-3368)* can express either coordination or adversative contrast ('but'), again context-dependent.

**Hypotactic (subordinate) constructions**, divided into complement clauses and adverbial clauses:

- **Complement clauses** are relatively rare and function as verb objects (most often with verbs of saying/perception); asyndetic (no connector) or syndetic, introduced by **ní** ('that'). Toward the end of the language's vitality, the Spanish borrowing **ke** ('that') increasingly replaces native *[ní](dictionary.html?q=n%C3%AD)* — direct textual evidence of ongoing Spanish-contact attrition.
- **Reported speech** has two strategies: (a) non-literal indirect speech as an object complement of *tek/te* 'to say'; (b) quoted/direct speech, most commonly introduced by the postposition **ka** ('to') in a construction where the main verb of saying is *omitted entirely* — e.g. *Mang [i'](dictionary.html#e-3409) [be](dictionary.html?q=be) ki ka, 'mí'rang ka, mí'rang ka.'* 'Then, to his mom: "Go, go."' — an elliptical quotative pattern that is one of Boruca's more distinctive discourse strategies.
- **Indirect questions** are typically marked with the Spanish complementizer *ke*, or (in one text) with Spanish *sábe* ('who knows') plus the connector *ang* — again showing heavy Spanish syntactic influence in this domain by the time of documentation.
- **Adverbial clauses** are classified into **seven types**, each with a dedicated (often disappearing/Spanish-replaced) marker:
  1. **Conditional**: originally marked by **angká** ('then/however') at the start of the apodosis (*Yó ki tru'rá, angká ógé róhk ki ya' sé-ra* 'If it rains, then everybody gets wet'), but by the time of these texts frequently replaced or supplemented by Spanish *[si](dictionary.html?q=si)* ('if'), showing a documented three-stage erosion: native marker only → native marker + Spanish *[si](dictionary.html?q=si)* → Spanish *[si](dictionary.html?q=si)* only.
  2. **Purposive**: implicit (no marker, second verb often bare or with optional infinitive *-í*) vs. explicit, marked by **chá** ('for, in order to') clause-initially and (usually) **yét** ('for') clause-finally — a discontinuous double-marking pattern unusual cross-linguistically; the second marker *yét* is sometimes dropped, and Spanish *a ver [si](dictionary.html?q=si)* ('to see if') is also attested intruding into this slot.
  3. **Causal**: originally marked by postposition **úge'** ('because') clause-finally, or by the compound demonstrative-focus-causal form **yabúge'** ('for that reason'); increasingly replaced by Spanish **porque**.
  4. **Temporal**: **néngwahk** ('first') for anteriority, **ki dengí** ('later') for posteriority; but the single most common way to express temporal sequence in running narrative is simple coordination with **mang** ('then').
  5. **Locative**: **yé'** clause-initially with postposition **ta** (sometimes replaceable by **ki**) clause-finally; Spanish **desde** ('from') is also attested intruding.
  6. **Simultaneous**: **í'k** ('when') clause-initially with **é'** clause-finally, though the second marker is often dropped, and Spanish **cuándo** is also widely attested as a direct substitute.
  7. **Causative/reason-giving** (a distinct category from causal, glossing roughly as "since"): the adverb **iní'** ('since') clause-initially, sometimes with **do'sh** ('like') clause-finally; can combine with a purposive clause in the same sentence.

Castro's overarching finding — echoed structurally throughout the paper — is a **clear, textually documented trajectory of Spanish syntactic encroachment** onto originally native subordination markers (conditional *[si](dictionary.html?q=si)*, causal *porque*, complementizer *ke*, temporal *cuándo*, locative *desde*), which is of direct relevance to revival planning (see §8): a grammar aiming to teach "traditional" Boruca subordination needs to consciously select the older native markers over the Hispanicized forms that appear in some of the very same recorded texts.

## 7. Cross-Chibchan Comparative Notes

Castro, Murillo & Quesada's "Evolución morfosintáctica en las lenguas chibchas" (2009) situates the Boruca tense→aspect shift (§5.2) alongside two parallel morphosyntactic evolutions in sister Chibchan languages, Teribe and Guaymí, arguing all three exemplify a broader tendency toward **"morphologization of grammatical relations"** — i.e., discourse/pragmatic patterns hardening into obligatory grammatical marking.

### 7.1 Teribe: from word order to head-marking morphology
Teribe (a close Isthmian-branch relative of Boruca) shows **three coexisting transitive clause orders**: basic SOV (used to open discourse and anchor/reinforce participant identity); the more frequent OV-s, where *-s* is now a grammaticalized person-indexing suffix on the verb (used in flowing/continuous discourse); and an **inverse construction** OVSdë, where the agent appears post-verbally marked by the obviative particle *dë* (used especially for 3rd-person-acting-on-3rd-person scenarios where the patient outranks the agent in discourse prominence/animacy). The authors argue, using both language-internal morphological evidence and Chibchan-family-external evidence (Pittier & Gagini's 1891 Teribe/Térraba data), that this inverse construction is a **reanalyzed former passive construction**: an original SOV-active/SV-passive system, in which the passive existed to avoid disallowed sequences of two same-paradigm pronominal forms and to topicalize patients, evolved as free postverbal pronouns cliticized onto the verb and became person-agreement suffixes, with the passive gradually restricted to (and reanalyzed as an inverse marking) situations where a non-discourse-participant (3rd person) acts on another 3rd person. Formal evidence for this includes fossilized 1st/2nd-person-agent "passive-marked" residues (e.g. *[Ta](dictionary.html?q=Ta) kimta-ga pãy dë* 'You help me', literally using inverse morphology despite a 1st-person patient) and binding-theory diagnostics showing the postverbal (obviative-marked) NP still functions as the syntactic subject despite its surface position.

### 7.2 Guaymí: emergence of a marked-nominative system
Panamanian Guaymí (per Kopesec 1975 and Quesada Pacheco 2008) shows a canonical **ergative-absolutive** pattern: the transitive subject (A) is marked with an ergative morpheme (four allomorphs: *kägwe*, *kä*, *gwe*, *kwe*, conditioned partly by whether the subject is a full NP, pronoun, or null/postverbal), while intransitive subjects (S) and objects (O) are unmarked. Ergative marking is suspended in present-tense and progressive constructions and optional with verbs of speech, command, and thought.

Costa Rican Guaymí, by contrast — based on the authors' own fieldwork and Payne (1982, 1997) — has **reorganized this system into a marked-nominative pattern**: the same morpheme family (now realized mainly as *gwe*, with *kwe* in free variation and postverbal position) marks subjects of *both* transitive and intransitive clauses (A and S alike), while objects remain unmarked — the mirror-image typological pattern from the Panamanian variety. The historically ergative form *kägwe* is unknown to Costa Rican speakers, and *kä* has been reduced to a purely lexical temporal/locative word ('time'/'place'). The authors treat this as evidence of an active, ongoing, geographically-conditioned reanalysis of the same etymological marker into typologically opposite alignment systems on either side of the Costa Rica–Panama border — a striking case study in rapid syntactic divergence within a single language.

### 7.3 Synthesis: a shared drift toward head-marking
The authors' summary table (their Cuadro 4) frames all three case studies as variants of a single areal/typological tendency toward increased **head-marking** (indexation of grammatical relations directly on the verb), achieved by different formal means in each language: in Boruca, via suffixation of the focus marker *ang* and personal pronouns onto finite verbs to express topic continuity (e.g., *at+ang* > *ang* '1SG-FOC', which the same paper documents fusing onto verbs, e.g. *So' Juana ang ohrí' sodíh-ra* 'Doña Juana makes tortillas'); in Teribe, via suffixation of person markers plus the creation of a wholly separate oblique/object pronoun paradigm; in Cuna, via *prefixation* of person pronouns in the original SOV order. The authors note that Boruca's own drift toward subject prefixation was interrupted by the language's extinction before the process could complete — placing the *ki*-clitic grammaticalization pathway described in §4 within this broader family-wide typological current, and underscoring that Boruca was mid-transition on *multiple* independent grammatical dimensions (TAM marking, subject indexing) at the moment documentation stopped.

## 8. Gaps and Open Questions for Revival Work

This section is an original synthesis assessment (not drawn from a single source) intended to flag, for materials-development purposes, where the documentary record is thin, internally contested, or likely to mislead a learner if taken at face value.

1. **Phonology was the single biggest gap; now partially resolved (2026).** *Largely resolved:* Quesada Pacheco (1994) — see §2.2 — is a genuine dedicated historical-comparative phonology, tracing 130+ years of sound change (the documented /pʃ/~/kʃ/ > /ʃ/ merger, confirmation of no native /l/, pretonic vowel reduction, tone-conditioned vowel alternation) across four independent 19th-century sources against the modern language. What it does *not* provide is a synchronic phonetic description of the modern language (minimal pairs, stress rules, precise articulatory detail, syllable structure) — that remains outside this corpus. Anyone building a learner-facing pronunciation guide will still need to go to Quesada Pacheco's *Hablemos [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)* (1995) teaching grammar, Constenla & Maroto's original description, or any surviving audio of the last semi-speakers for that synchronic layer.

2. **The tense-vs-aspect debate is unresolved, not settled.** Castro's aspectual reanalysis (§5.2) is well-argued and independently corroborated by Abarca González's frequency data (the near-total absence of *-kirá*), but it is explicitly framed by its own author as a *proposal*, not a consensus — the original tense analysis of Constenla & Maroto and Quesada Pacheco remains the majority position in the earlier literature and is the one embedded in existing teaching materials (e.g., *Hablemos [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)*, whose abbreviations — PrAF "present affirmative," PfAF "preterite affirmative," etc. — assume the older tense framework; note Quesada Pacheco continued using tense-labeled glosses as late as the 2011 causativity paper). A revival curriculum should probably pick one analysis explicitly and say so, rather than silently mixing tense-labeled and aspect-labeled terminology drawn from different sources — the two glossing systems are not directly interchangeable and mixing them will confuse both instructors and any linguistically sophisticated learners.

3. **The infinitive's distribution is acknowledged as unexplained.** Castro is explicit that the intermittent appearance of *-í/-íh* in near-identical contexts has "no consistency" she could identify from the data, and flags a possible phonological conditioning as a hypothesis for future research. This means any pedagogical rule taught for "when to use the infinitive" will necessarily be a simplification not yet grounded in a confirmed grammatical rule.

4. **The adjective suffix -cá is mentioned but never actually described.** Quesada Pacheco's semantic hierarchy for adjectives (§3.8) posits a [– process] suffix *-cá* as the structural counterpart to *-rani*, and gives a few contrastive examples (*ejtcá* 'thin (currently)' vs. *[ejdát](dictionary.html#e-3238)* 'thin (inherently)'), but the paper never devotes a numbered section to *-cá*'s own semantic range or productivity the way it does for the other six suffixes. This is a clear content gap for a complete adjective-morphology unit.

5. **Grade/intensity morphology is described as actively decaying within living memory.** Quesada Pacheco explicitly reports informants confusing the attenuative (*-rani*) and intensive (*-ón/*) grades, and interprets reinforced forms like *cuín/crasón/* ('very very-resistant') in Maroto's manuscripts as evidence the system was already breaking down generations before the language's final extinction. A revival grammar should probably present this system with an explicit caveat that even the last fluent-ish speakers were inconsistent about it, rather than presenting it as a stable, fully productive paradigm.

6. **Causative productivity claims rest on a very small, non-representative sample.** The morphological causative in *-óv* is attested for only three verbs; the "unique" suprasegmental tonal causative is explicitly described by its own author as lexicalized/non-productive and was rejected by at least one informant for extension to new verbs. Given that the entire causativity study relies on direct elicitation from a dying-language population (per the paper's own methodological note), frequency/productivity claims here should be treated as suggestive rather than statistically robust — unlike the predicate-suffix frequencies in §5.4, which are based on a substantial 1,516-token natural-narrative corpus and are comparatively much more trustworthy as a basis for sequencing.

7. **No genuine ergativity/subject-marking study of Boruca exists in this corpus.** The one source with "sujeto y ergativo" in its title (Wilson, undated) turns out to contain no Boruca data whatsoever — it is purely comparative/theoretical and Bribri-focused. This means Boruca's actual argument-alignment system (word order + discourse-conditioned *ki*/*ang*/*[abí](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD)* clitics, as described in §4 and referenced in Castro 2012's footnote 6) has not received a dedicated, focused syntactic study of the kind Wilson's title promised. This is a significant documentation gap, since argument marking is foundational for teaching sentence construction. *Partially resolved (2026):* Castro (2008) does give an actual argument for alignment (see §6.1) — coreference and coordination diagnostics supporting **nominative-accusative** alignment, with *ang* restricted to subject function and *ki* covering subject function plus nominal-only (not pronominal) object function. This is real Boruca-specific evidence, but Castro herself frames it as preliminary ("in future papers, a more thorough analysis will be made"), so the gap is narrowed, not closed — a fuller dedicated study remains a documentation priority. *Further resolved (2026):* Quesada (2000) — see §4.3 — is exactly the "dedicated, focused" study this gap calls for, though framed as grammaticalization theory rather than alignment typology: it gives the formal mechanism (DEFINITE ARTICLE > SPECIFIER > SUBJECT MARKER) and the specific reason *ki* landed on subjects rather than objects (disambiguating syncretic subject/object pronoun forms), plus full distributional tables by grammatical relation and semantic case role. Between Castro's alignment argument and Quesada's grammaticalization account, this gap is now substantially — though still not completely — closed; what remains missing is a study that puts the two analyses in direct dialogue with each other, since they were written independently and never cross-cite. *A third piece added (2026):* J.D. Quesada (1996) — see new §4.4 — completes the *ki*/*[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)*/*[abí](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD)* triad with a dedicated study of *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)* specifically, resolving the terminological split between Castro's "focus marker ang" (§6.1, §7.3) and the older "destematizer añ" label (§4.3) in favor of the former, and adding a previously undocumented subject-relativizer function. The gap is now about as closed as this corpus permits: all three subject-marking clitics have at least one dedicated focused study, though (a) the three studies still don't cross-cite each other and (b) Quesada (1996) himself flags one unresolved counterexample (a focus-fronted postpositional phrase not triggering *[añ](dictionary.html?q=a%C3%B1)*) as an open problem.

8. **The negation-marker system is analyzed two incompatible ways across the corpus, with no adjudication.** Quesada Pacheco's *Hablemos [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)* (1995) treats negation as TAM-conditioned — a distinct negative marker for each tense/aspect category — while Castro (2008) argues for a single uniform negative suffix, *í'shi* (surface variants phonologically conditioned), and characterizes the per-TAM analysis as possibly reflecting "an erroneous analysis of the data" (see §6.1). Neither position is definitively resolved within this corpus, and no source here contains enough raw negated-clause tokens across TAM categories to independently adjudicate. A revival curriculum needs to pick one analysis and state its choice explicitly, since teaching "one negator per tense" versus "one negator, period" are pedagogically very different claims, and mixing the two source's terminology silently would confuse learners the same way the tense-vs-aspect mixing flagged in gap 2 would.

9. **Spanish-contact attrition is pervasive and needs an explicit editorial policy.** Castro's complex-clause paper documents, with textual evidence, a real-time replacement of native subordinators (conditional, causal, complementizer, temporal, locative) by their Spanish equivalents within the very corpus of "traditional" narratives used for documentation. A revival grammar drawing example sentences from these same narrative collections (Constenla & Maroto 1979/1986; Quesada Pacheco 1996) will unavoidably encounter mixed native/Spanish-calque sentences and should decide, and state clearly to users, whether it is teaching the older native forms, the attested "living" (Spanish-influenced) forms actually used by the last speakers, or both with commentary — silently presenting one recorded sentence as "correct traditional Boruca" without flagging its Spanish elements risks teaching hispanized forms as if they were the older grammatical norm, or vice versa.

10. **Cross-source terminological inconsistency.** The eight sources, spanning nearly two decades of scholarship by overlapping but not identical author teams, use different glossing conventions and even different romanizations for the same forms (e.g., *ki* vs. *[quí](dictionary.html?q=qu%C3%AD)*; *-crá* vs. *-krá*; *n̈* vs. *η* for the velar nasal). A synthesis grammar or teaching curriculum will need to standardize orthography and glossing conventions explicitly, since simply pooling examples verbatim (as this document does, for source fidelity) will otherwise present an inconsistent surface appearance to learners. *Partially addressed*: the glottal stop specifically has now been standardized to the apostrophe (') for this project — see §2.1. The velar nasal, tone marking, and other romanization variants (noted above in this same point) remain open and should be resolved the same way: by checking which notation is already best-attested in the underlying data and in current community usage, rather than defaulting to whichever source happens to be cited last.

11. **No connected-text longer than the single narrative appendix in Castro (2010) is available in this source set.** Only "The Complex Clause in Boruca" includes a full interlinear text with free translation (the *Wá' yabá* legend). This is invaluable and should be mined further for illustrative material, but a single ~250-word text is a thin base for demonstrating natural discourse-level patterns (e.g., how frequently different subordinator types actually co-occur in real storytelling) — supplementing with the fuller Constenla & Maroto (1979/1986) and Quesada Pacheco (1996) primary text collections, which several of these papers draw examples from but do not reproduce in full, would substantially strengthen any revival curriculum's example base. *Largely resolved (2026):* this project has since recovered several substantial connected texts not available when this gap was first identified — Rojas Chaves's full trilingual "La vieja Shécra" legend (~39 lines, `boruca_shecra_full_text.md`), five Constenla-recorded songs (`boruca_songs_raw.json`), roughly 23,500 words of parallel Spanish/Boruca radio-script translations by Maroto Rojas himself (`boruca_maroto_articulos_periodisticos.md`), and — larger than all of the above combined — the complete bilingual **"Tanu" novela**, a sustained ~40,000-word narrative with plot and recurring characters, plus its own extensive footnote series (`boruca_maroto_tanu_novela.md`). Between the two Maroto Rojas prose corpora alone this project now has over 60,000 words of genuine mid-20th-century naturalistic connected Boruca prose. A future revision of this grammar should draw its illustrative discourse-level examples from this material rather than relying on the single Castro appendix — the *Tanu* novela in particular, with its sustained narrative arc and footnoted textual variants ("otra versión" notes throughout), is a strong candidate for the kind of dedicated discourse-level study this gap originally called for.

12. **A concrete, attributable model for lexical elaboration/neologism strategy now exists but is not yet incorporated into this grammar.** Maroto Rojas's own presentación (in the same 1999 volume) documents his translation methodology with worked examples — semantic extension of existing roots, transparent compounding, and, for specifically technical/scientific vocabulary, outright borrowing from Spanish with only Boruca plural/case morphology attached (see `boruca_maroto_rojas_synthesis.md` §4 and §10). This is the most concrete primary-source evidence anywhere in the project's corpus for how an actual native speaker coined or imported new vocabulary, and should be the empirical starting point — rather than a purely theoretical exercise — whenever the still-undrafted neologism framework (referenced in `boruca_language_council_proposal.md`) is finally written. It likely also belongs as a new subsection of `boruca_word_formation.md`, not just this grammar.

## 9. Kinship Terminology (2026 addition)

Adolfo Constenla Umaña's "La terminología de parentesco de los indios borucas" (1977, based on 1975 fieldwork; full synthesis in `boruca_constenla_1977_kinship_synthesis.md`) is the only dedicated kinship study in this project's corpus, and — notably — predates every other grammatical source reviewed here by at least a decade, making it also the earliest linguistic fieldwork represented in this project outside the 19th-century manuscript sources (§2.2).

**System typology.** Ego's own generation follows a **Hawaiian-type** pattern (maternal and paternal cousins terminologically equated with each other and with siblings), a pattern that extends into the 2nd/3rd ascending and (as far as observed) 2nd/3rd descending generations. Two generations break this otherwise-generational logic: the **1st ascending generation is lineal** (uncles/aunts are equated with each other across maternal/paternal lines, but distinguished from parents), and the **1st descending generation** distinguishes ego's own children from the children of ego's siblings. No sex distinction is made in ascending generations beyond parents/grandparents. The one point in the entire system where a distinct lexical choice tracks marriage rather than blood/generation is the husband/wife pair *cóñát*/*[ramát](dictionary.html#e-3602)* (see below).

**Formal patterns worth cross-referencing with §2–§3 above.** Third-generation kinship terms (great-grandparent, great-grandchild) are formed by prefixing **du-** to the second-generation term (*[só'](dictionary.html#e-3847) 'abuela' → [dusó'](dictionary.html#e-3219) 'bisabuela'*; *xasúj 'abuelo' → [duxasúj](dictionary.html#e-3224) 'bisabuelo'*; *[bríshba](dictionary.html?q=br%C3%ADshba) 'nieto' → dubríshba 'bisnieto'*) — this is the *same* prefix independently documented by Rojas Chaves (1992; see `boruca_word_formation.md` §4 and `boruca_rojas_chaves_1992_synthesis.md` §4), now attested from a second, earlier (1977) source, reinforcing that this is a genuine, if minor and lexically closed, exception to Boruca's otherwise near-exclusively suffixing derivational profile. Several kinship terms (*[cabát](dictionary.html?q=cab%C3%A1t)* 'hermano/primo', *[ramát](dictionary.html#e-3602)* 'esposa/hembra', *cóñát* 'esposo/macho') are built on the suffix **-át**, which Constenla (1977) already identifies as "uno de los más frecuentes en la derivación de nombres adjetivos y sustantivos" — independent 1977 corroboration, predating this project's other productivity evidence by nearly two decades, that *-át* was Boruca's most productive derivational suffix (cf. `boruca_word_formation.md` §3's own database count of 33 headwords, and Rojas Chaves's 1992 classification of *-át* as one of only 11 "muy productivos" suffixes).

**Comparative Chibchan cognates**, extending this project's family-comparative evidence (§7) into the lexical domain: *yuéjt* 'padre' ~ Bribri *yé*; *[be](dictionary.html?q=be)* 'madre' ~ Térraba *mé*, Bribri *amí*, Matagalpa *amis*; *[cabát](dictionary.html?q=cab%C3%A1t)* 'hermano' ~ Bribri *kabö* (a term of endearment used by female cousins for male cousins); *[ramát](dictionary.html#e-3602)* 'esposa' ~ Bribri *alakölö*, Cabécar *alágla* 'mujer'; *duá* 'cuñado' ~ Bribri *dua'*, Cabécar *dawa'*; *[bo'c](dictionary.html#e-2256)* 'cuñada' ~ Térraba *bó*; *[huá'](dictionary.html?q=hu%C3%A1%27)* 'hijo/a' — cited as part of a very widely-attested Americas-wide root, paralleled in Quechua *[huahua](dictionary.html?q=huahua)* and Lenca *guagua* ('nursing infant'); *sícua* 'extranjero' ~ Bribri/Cabécar/Térraba *sikua*/*sigua*; *[abí](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD)* 'persona' ~ Bribri *apé'* 'no pariente'.

**Ethnonyms — a direct, much earlier corroboration of already-documented vocabulary.** Constenla (1977) records the same **abí + color-term** compounding pattern for ethnic/linguistic categories already documented from vocabulary-database evidence in `boruca_word_formation.md` §2.6: *[abí suát](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD%20su%C3%A1t)* 'persona blanca (hispana)', *[abí](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD) brit* 'persona negra (jamaiquino)', *[abí drubát](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD%20drub%C3%A1t)* 'persona colorada — blanco no hispanohablante, especialmente anglosajón'. Finding this exact three-way distinction, including the same non-obvious "colorada/anglosajón" extension, in a source from **1977** — roughly half a century before the vocabulary database was compiled — is strong independent confirmation that this classifier-compounding pattern for naming post-contact social categories was already fully established generations before this project's other evidence for it. A related, smaller finding: a synonym for 'borucas' recorded alongside the still-current *[brúñcajc](dictionary.html#e-2328) [rójc](dictionary.html?q=r%C3%B3jc)* — namely **brúñshuá rójc** (< *[shuá'](dictionary.html#e-4105)* 'desechos') — was already noted as more precise but disused ("desusada") by 1975, a documented case of lexical obsolescence within the ethnonym system itself, worth setting alongside this grammar's other Spanish-contact-attrition evidence (§8, gap 9).

**Vocabulary-database cross-check.** Nearly every term recorded by Constenla (1977) — the kinship terms, the ethnonyms, and the broader person-reference vocabulary (*[ramrójc](dictionary.html#e-3604)* 'mujer', *[cóñrójc](dictionary.html#e-2606)* 'hombre', *do'uá* 'casado', *ijnéñ* 'soltero', *sayú'í* 'amigo/peón', *[shiduá](dictionary.html#e-4010)* 'novio/a') — was checked against `boruca_vocabulary.db` during this pass and found **already present**, generally with closely matching glosses. This is a valuable independent-corroboration finding in its own right (a 1977 academic source and the database's own, much-later, largely Maroto-Rojas-derived material converge closely on this entire semantic domain) but means little genuinely new vocabulary was available to add; no new database insertions were made from this source, consistent with this project's practice of not duplicating well-corroborated existing entries.

**Ethnographic content** (incest-taboo narratives, kinship behavioral norms) from this same source has been folded into `boruca_historical_periods.md` rather than here, as it fits that document's existing ethnographic material better than this grammar's structural focus.

**Deeper protochibchan etymologies, from a tangential comparative source (2026 addition).** Constenla Umaña's broader protochibchan reconstruction paper (2008, "Subclasificación de las lenguas chibchenses y del protochibchense," *Estudios de Lingüística Chibcha* 27; full synthesis in `boruca_tangential_chibchan_papers_synthesis.md`) — a general comparative-Chibchan source, not a dedicated Boruca study — extends two etymologies already in this project's materials. First, it confirms and sharpens the *-crá*/*-cuá* suffix derivation from protochibchan */kada³/* 'tree/stick/elongated object' and */kua²/* 'seed/round object' (already noted via Constenla 1977/1991 above), specifying that Boruca is one of the family's languages where these etyma survive **only** as bound suffixes, having lost all independent free-form use — unlike some sister languages, where a cognate free form persists alongside the grammaticalized suffix. Second, it gives a protochibchan source for the 1PL pronoun *dì'* 'nos, nuestro' (§3 above): protochibchan */di/* plus a grammaticalizing formative, the same source that yields movere's cognate *ni* 'nosotros (inclusivo)' — a small piece of comparative-historical depth for a pronoun form this project previously had only synchronically.

# Word Formation in Boruca

*A synthesis of how [Boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474) actually builds words — from the documented derivational grammar (reference grammar §3–§5, Appendix B) plus a fresh empirical pass over the 2,255-entry vocabulary database. This [is](dictionary.html?q=is) a prerequisite study for the neologism framework: before proposing rules for coining new words, we need to know which processes the language demonstrably used, how productively, and on what kind of source material.*

## 1. Why mine the vocabulary database, not just the grammar papers

The reference grammar's suffix inventory (Appendix B) came from a handful of illustrative examples in each grammar paper — enough to establish that a process exists, not enough to show how hard it actually works across the lexicon. The vocabulary database is a different kind of evidence: 2,255 entries compiled from field elicitation and community sources, largely independent of the grammar papers' own example sentences. Patterns that show up repeatedly *there* are patterns a coinage method can lean on with more confidence.

Headline finding: **443 of 2,255 headwords (19.6%) are multi-word compounds.** Compounding, not suffixation, is the single largest word-formation process visible in the corpus by raw count — which reframes the whole neologism question. The *-uá* 'camera'/'refrigerator' pattern cited earlier is real and productive, but it's actually the smaller of the two major strategies Boruca uses to name new things.

---

## 1.5 How deep do these patterns go? Boruca within proto-Chibchan (2026 addition)

Everything in §2–§6 documents *how* Boruca builds words. This section addresses a different question raised directly by the project: how far back do these devices actually go, and did Boruca keep changing on its own after it split off from its relatives? Full analysis, sourcing, and caveats in `boruca_protochibchan_development_synthesis.md`; summary here.

**The depth.** Constenla Umaña's classification places Boruca as its own undivided branch (B.2.1.3, Ístmico occidental) coordinate with Viceítico (bribri+cabécar) and Naso (teribe/térraba) — not nested inside either. His own glottochronological estimates (2011: 96-97) date the split of proto-Ístmico — the last common ancestor Boruca shares with bribri, cabécar, and teribe/térraba — to roughly **4,600 years ago**, with the deeper proto-Chibchan-meridional node (ancestor of the vótico, ístmico, and magdalénico branches) at roughly 5,400 years, and the very deepest reconstructable node (protolenmichí, the shared ancestor of Chibchan, Lenca, and Misumalpa) at roughly 9,700 years. For scale: Constenla notes this is nearly double the depth of the oldest split in the entire Mayan language family (~2,500 years, Kaufman 1974). These are glottochronological estimates with real uncertainty (±1,105 years on the oldest date), not fixed facts — but even taken as order-of-magnitude, they establish that whatever word-formation machinery Boruca demonstrably kept from this ancestry predates the first European contact by a margin about a thousand times longer than the contact period itself.

**What demonstrably survived that gap.** Two of the suffixes central to §3 above are not Boruca-internal inventions: Constenla (2008) traces **-crá** to protochibchan */kada³/* 'tree, stick, elongated cylindrical object' and **-cua** to */kua²/* 'seed, round object' — Boruca is one of the languages where both survive only as bound suffixes, having lost all independent free-form use. The 1PL pronoun *dì'* traces to proto */di/* + a grammaticalizing formative, cognate with movere's (Ngäbe's) *ni*. Separately, this project's own comparative work (task #54, `boruca_thiel_stone_comparative_synthesis.md`) found the *dí'* 'water' classifier (§2.2 above) and the *suñ*/*[suturíc](dictionary.html#e-3946)* 'deer' root (§2.3 above) both have direct cognates in bribri and cabécar — plausible further evidence these are old Ístmico-wide inheritances rather than Boruca innovations, though this cognate-scan work is a shallower gloss-matching method than Constenla's own reconstruction and shouldn't be weighted equally.

**What Boruca changed on its own, recently and observably.** Castro, Murillo & Quesada (2009/2011, "Evolución morfosintáctica en las lenguas chibchas" — a source newly processed for this addition) document two grammatical changes that were *still actively in progress* within Boruca's own documented lifespan (Pittier's 1890s texts through Quesada Pacheco's 1990s–2000s terminal-speaker fieldwork), not reconstructed from deep comparison: (1) the core TAM markers *-ra/-irá/-krá/-kirá*, traditionally analyzed as a **tense** system, show concrete textual and frequency evidence of reorganizing into an **aspectual** system instead — including one of the last semi-speakers, Eligia Maroto, reportedly not recognizing or using *-kirá* at all; (2) the independent focus particle *ang* was progressively fusing onto personal pronouns (*at+ang→ang*, *i+ang→ing*, etc.) and those fused forms were beginning to further cliticize onto finite verbs to mark topic continuity (*krí'-ír-ing* 'was [topic-continued]') — Boruca's own homegrown route toward head-marking morphology, paralleled by different mechanisms in Teribe (passive-to-inverse reanalysis) and Cuna (prefix consolidation). This is genuinely rare evidence in historical linguistics: a grammatical change caught mid-transition by the documentary record, not just inferred from a "before" and "after" centuries apart.

**Why this matters here.** The classifier-compounding template and *-crá*/*-cua* suffixation aren't just old — they're old on a scale that dwarfs the entire documented history of Boruca-Spanish contact, which gives a modern coinage using them real continuity with pre-contact word-formation, not just "following inherited rules." And the tense-to-aspect and pronoun-fusion findings matter for a different reason: they're direct evidence Boruca's grammar was still actively, internally changing right up to the point transmission broke, which is relevant precedent for a modernization program that wants to make deliberate systematic choices rather than treating every historically-attested form as equally frozen and equally "correct." See §7, point 10, for the practical takeaway.

## 2. Compounding: the dominant process

Compounding in Boruca strongly favors **[generic head noun] + [specifier]** structure, where a small set of broad, semantically "bleached" nouns function almost like classifiers, combining productively with a specifier (another noun, an adjective, or occasionally a verb) to name a whole category of related concepts. This is directly evidenced, not inferred:

### 2.1 các ('mundo, sol, tiempo, día, rayo, trueno, cielo' — world/sun/time/day/sky) as a temporal classifier
| Compound | Gloss |
|---|---|
| các ba'cá | amanecer ('dawn') |
| các bejdé | 'to be late (~5pm)' |
| các bigát | noche ('night') |
| các bigát bujtá | medianoche ('midnight') |
| các boc | relámpago, nube ('lightning, cloud') |
| các bujtá | mediodía ('midday') |

**Rule visible here:** all divisions of the day/sky/weather are built by taking the broad head *[các](dictionary.html?q=c%C3%A1c)* and specifying it — a single polysemous root ('world/sun/time/sky') anchors an entire semantic field rather than each concept getting an unrelated root.

**A historical example of this same classifier, now opacified (2026 addition).** Quesada Pacheco (1994, comparing the 19th-century manuscript sources) traces modern *kagtú* 'devil' back to a two-morpheme compound — *ká* 'day, time' (the same *[các](dictionary.html?q=c%C3%A1c)*-family root as above) + *brú* 'chief, leader' — visible directly in the 19th-century spellings: Valentini *kabru*, Gabb *kag brú*, Thiel *[ca](dictionary.html?q=ca) buru*, Pittier *kag bru*. The second element, *brú*/*buru*, has since dropped out of Boruca as an independently usable word, so modern speakers perceive *kagtú* as a single unanalyzable root rather than a compound. This is the clearest example in this project's corpus of **compound-to-simplex opacification** — a compound losing its internal transparency specifically because one of its component morphemes fell out of use elsewhere in the language — and is worth flagging as a fourth process alongside compounding, derivation, and valence-marking, distinct from the synchronically-live classifier pattern documented just above it.

### 2.2 dí' ('water') as a hydrological/liquid classifier
dí' bu's 'confluence point of a river', dí' cajc 'small river/creek', dí' sá 'headwaters', dí' shú'c 'water duck', dí' yu'crá 'well, pool', dí'suá 'goose/waterfowl'. Fifteen distinct compounds attested, covering both natural water features and water-associated animals — *dí'* functions as a productive classifier for 'anything defined by its relationship to water.'

**Cross-Chibchan corroboration (2026 addition).** A systematic cognate scan across Thiel's 1882 survey — which used a single master Spanish gloss list across Boruca, the Talamanca-family languages, and Guatuso, making cross-list comparison methodologically sound rather than coincidental — finds Boruca *dii*/*[di](dictionary.html?q=di)* matching bribri/cabécar *[di](dictionary.html?q=di)*/*dij*/*dicru*/*dicruh* across five independent glosses ('boca de río', 'río pequeño', 'orilla de río', 'río grande', 'jugo'). This confirms the *dí'* classifier documented above reflects an old, shared Isthmian-Chibchan root rather than a Boruca-internal innovation. See `boruca_thiel_stone_comparative_synthesis.md` §3 for the full analysis.

### 2.3 turi ('ganado' — cattle) as a livestock classifier
turi bac 'rancher', turi ca' 'cow's milk', turi crabé'íj 'herder/cowboy', turi ramát 'cow (female)', turi sá' 'slaughterhouse', turi sujgrójc 'bull', turi ú 'stable' (*[ú](dictionary.html#e-4380)* = 'house', so literally 'cattle-house'). This entire semantic domain — a domain that necessarily post-dates Spanish contact, since cattle are a colonial-era introduction to the Americas — was built by compounding onto a single borrowed-or-adapted head noun, rather than borrowing a separate Spanish word for every cattle-related concept. **This is direct historical evidence that Boruca handled a genuinely new domain of concepts (post-contact livestock ranching) through internal compounding, not wholesale lexical borrowing** — exactly the kind of precedent a neologism method for modern technology concepts should point to.

**Note on the unrelated *[turi](dictionary.html?q=turi)*/*[suturíc](dictionary.html#e-3946)* 'deer' root (2026 addition).** The *[turi](dictionary.html?q=turi)* 'cattle' classifier documented above is a distinct, homophonous-only root from the *[turi](dictionary.html?q=turi)*/*[suturíc](dictionary.html#e-3946)* element in *[suturíc](dictionary.html#e-3946)* 'venado' (deer) discussed via Rojas Chaves (1992) elsewhere in this document. That deer root is now triply corroborated: (1) Rojas Chaves (1992)'s own etymological argument tracing it to a protochibchan *\*suñ* root; (2) an independent Thiel-era cross-list scan finding [Boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474) *su* ~ bribri *suni* ~ cabécar *sumi* for the same gloss; (3) Doris Stone's Appendix C table, where 'deer' [is](dictionary.html?q=is) one of the most stable entries across the entire 1862–1945 record (Valentini *suturik*, Thiel 1920 *suturíc*, Pittier 1941 *su-turik*, Stone 1945 *suturik*) — and the modern dictionary's *suturíc* is essentially identical to Thiel's 1920 form, indicating at least 144, likely 164, years of phonetic stability for this word. See `boruca_thiel_stone_comparative_synthesis.md` §2–3 for the full cross-source table.

### 2.4 ca'ísh ('cara' — face) as a facial-anatomy classifier
ca'ísh cuá's 'eyelid', ca'ísh cúp 'eye', ca'ísh é'ua 'mask', ca'ísh shá's 'pupil', ca'ísh shíj 'eyelash/eyebrow'. Face-part anatomy is compositionally derived from 'face' + specifier, rather than each part having an unrelated root.

### 2.5 rán'cra ('field/plantation of —') as a cultivation-site classifier
This is the cleanest, most rule-like pattern in the whole corpus — a closed, fully regular set: bajyrá rán'cra 'cañaveral' (sugarcane field), briduá rán'cra 'bananal' (banana grove), cáu' rán'cra 'cacaotal' (cacao grove), chubú rán'cra 'algodonal' (cotton field), mun rán'cra 'platanal' (plantain grove), rí' rán'cra 'milpa/maizal' (cornfield), surá rán'cra 'barrial' (mud flat). **[crop noun] + rán'cra = 'field of [crop]', with no exceptions found in the data.** This is as close to a productive derivational rule as anything in the corpus, and a template any new "field of X" coinage could safely reuse.

### 2.6 abí ('person, people') + adjective → descriptive ethnonym/person-category compounds
abí brít 'black person' (brít 'negro'), abí drubát 'red/colored person; light-skinned non-Spanish-speaker, esp. of Saxon/Germanic origin' (drubát 'red', cf. Appendix A adjective list), abí suát 'white/Hispanic person' (suát 'white'). Notably *[abí drubát](dictionary.html?q=ab%C3%AD%20drub%C3%A1t)*'s gloss shows the pattern extending to a genuinely modern ethnic/linguistic distinction (Spanish-speaking vs. non-Spanish-speaking European-descended people) — further evidence this compounding strategy was still productively used to name new social categories encountered after contact, not a frozen archaic pattern.

### 2.7 A smaller, less certain classifier: bóc / xít
bóc appears in bóc shuán' 'ash-colored squirrel', bóc suát 'nieve' (snow — likely 'white squirrel' used metaphorically/descriptively for an unfamiliar tropical-latitude phenomenon), bóc tuját 'hielo' (ice), bóc xít 'flying squirrel'. xít appears independently in bót xít 'revolver' (bót = tube/weapon root, cf. bót cúp 'blowgun'), bú'xa xít 'a bird species', sibú xít 'pupil of the eye' (lit. plausibly 'eye's small-one'). The pattern is real but the semantic contribution of *xít* is not confidently resolved from this data alone (possibly 'small, young, quick, offspring-of') — flagged as a genuine open question rather than asserted, since a neologism method should not build on classifier meanings we're not sure of.

### 2.8 A second syntactic (not morphological) naming strategy: lexicalized possessive phrases (2026 addition)

Rojas Chaves (1992), in a dedicated 94-suffix study of Boruca derivational morphology (see `boruca_rojas_chaves_1992_synthesis.md` for the full inventory), makes an important structural point that complicates the "classifier compounding is the dominant strategy" picture in §2.1–2.6 above: she argues Boruca actually prefers **syntactic possessive phrases** over morphological compounding for naming complex concepts — a genitive-like "[part] of [whole]" construction, not a single fused word. Her anatomy examples: *[ca'ísh cúp](dictionary.html#e-2550)* 'pupila' lit. 'seed of the eye', *[ca'ísh cuá's](dictionary.html#e-2549)* 'párpado' lit. 'skin of the eye', *[cása cuá'scua](dictionary.html#e-2522)* 'labio' lit. 'skin of the mouth', *[is cása](dictionary.html#e-3391)* 'ano' lit. 'mouth of the rear'. She draws an explicit parallel to Kaufman's (1974) Mayan examples (*'vena' = 'camino de la sangre'*, *'pulgar' = 'madre de la mano'*). For a neologism framework, this means a new concept doesn't always need a single fused word — a transparent possessive phrase built from two already-known nouns is itself a legitimate, attested Boruca strategy, and in Rojas Chaves's assessment may in fact be the *more* frequently used one, even though the classifier-compound pattern in §2.1–2.6 is the one that shows up most clearly as fused headwords in the vocabulary database.

A small, related compounding fact from the same source: **sa-** 'cabeza' (head, cf. *[sagrá](dictionary.html#e-3730)* 'cabeza' via -crá) also compounds with theme-level nouns — *sa- + tebéc* 'serpiente' → *satebéc* 'serpiente tamagaz' (fer-de-lance) — and with roots meaning 'abnormality' to derive sensory-deficit adjectives: *cua-* 'oído' (hearing) + *dú'-* 'abnormality' → *cuadú'* 'sordo' (deaf); *[shis](dictionary.html?q=shis)* 'moco/nariz' (mucus/nose) + *dú'-* → *[shisdú'](dictionary.html#e-4044)* 'gangoso' (nasal speech). This is a small, genuine addition to the classifier-head inventory in §2.1–2.6, though with only 3 attested examples it isn't yet established as a fully productive pattern the way *[các](dictionary.html?q=c%C3%A1c)*, *dí'*, *[turi](dictionary.html?q=turi)*, *[ca'ísh](dictionary.html?q=ca%27%C3%ADsh)*, and *rán'cra* are.

## 3. Suffixal derivation (confirms and quantifies the grammar-paper findings)

Corpus-wide counts of headwords ending in each documented adjectival/nominal suffix (Appendix B):

| Suffix | Headwords ending in it | Function |
|---|---|---|
| -shi | 38 | positional/stative adjective |
| -át | 33 | inherent-quality adjective (confirmed as the most productive adjectival suffix, consistent with §3.5) |
| -uá | 29 | instrument nominalizer — see below |
| -ón/ | 2 | intensive degree (confirms §3.4's note that this suffix is lexically restricted, not broadly productive) |
| -rani | 0 | attenuative — **zero exact-suffix matches in the vocabulary database itself**; all attestations of this suffix in the corpus come from the grammar papers' own elicited examples, not the community dictionary. This sharpens the §8 gap-flag: *-rani* may be even less synchronically active than previously stated. |

The **-uá instrument/nominalizer** pattern (already known from *[huic](dictionary.html?q=huic) chijguá* 'camera', *seján sádijuá* 'refrigerator', *[di'](dictionary.html?q=di%27) brojguá* 'coffee-maker') is independently corroborated by 29 vocabulary entries, including *[brá'uá](dictionary.html#e-2276)* 'storage; a machine used to make skeins of cotton' — a second, independently attested case of *-uá* being used to name a piece of technology/machinery, not just borrowed loanwords. This is good evidence the pattern is genuinely available for naming tools and machines, old or new.

**A dedicated 38-suffix inventory, now folded in (2026 addition).** Rojas Chaves (1992) — a full derivational-morphology study, not an incidental grammar-paper example set (full inventory in `boruca_rojas_chaves_1992_synthesis.md`) — independently documents *-uá* (there glossed as marking instrument nouns/resultative adjectives from verbs, e.g. *dúbra'* 'amansar' → *dúbra'uá* 'manso') and *-cá*, *-át*, *-shi* among her 11 suffixes she classifies as "muy productivos" (highly productive), corroborating this table's own count-based productivity ranking from an independent 1992 source predating this project's vocabulary-database compilation. She adds several further highly-productive suffixes not broken out separately in this document's own database count (because they're rarer in this particular 2,255-entry sample than in her broader source material): **-cráñ** (possession/abundance, e.g. *cuá* 'piojo' → *[cuacráñ](dictionary.html#e-2768)* 'piojoso'), **-cra** (tree/plant species and tools, e.g. *bíjt-* → *[bíjtcra](dictionary.html?q=b%C3%ADjtcra)* 'hacha'; also the place-of-action sense, e.g. *[cahuí'](dictionary.html?q=cahu%C3%AD%27)* 'vivir' → *[cahuí'cra](dictionary.html#e-2465)* 'vivienda'), **-í~-ij** (agent nouns, e.g. *[huí](dictionary.html#e-3323)* 'saber' → *huiíj~huií* 'sabio'), **-óñ/-raní** (the intense/attenuated grades of the three-way intensity system already noted above as *-ón'*/rare in the database), and **-ráñ** (a second body-part-noun suffix, e.g. *bid-* → *[bidráñ](dictionary.html#e-2191)* 'pecho'). Her quantitative finding that Boruca suffixation overwhelmingly favors **category transfer** (exocentric derivation, changing part of speech) over within-category modification — 19 of 36 secondary-derivation suffixes are exocentric-only, vs. only 10 endocentric-only — is a useful general principle for a neologism method: expect a new suffix-derived word to usually belong to a *different* word class than its base, not the same one.

## 4. Valence-changing and argument-structure marking (already documented, now cross-checked)

The causative/anticausative system (reference grammar §5.3) is a word-formation-adjacent process worth keeping in the same frame as compounding and suffixation, since coining a new *verb* (not just a noun) will usually mean choosing among: bare lexical alternation (no marking), the *-óv* suffix (attested unproductive, only 3 verbs), analytic auxiliaries (*sodíj*, *ón̈*, *shún̈* — the productive, currently-preferred strategy), or the tonal alternation (lexicalized, not extendable to new roots per Quesada Pacheco's own informant-rejection test). **For neologism purposes, this means new causative verbs should default to the analytic/auxiliary strategy**, which is both the most productive attested pattern and the easiest to teach/learn, rather than attempting suffixal or tonal causativization on new roots.

A separate, previously unremarked pattern surfaced in this pass: a number of vocabulary entries show a verb root followed by a parenthetical postposition marking its argument frame — e.g. *[babán' (úgue')](dictionary.html#e-46)* 'to trade (for)', *[bia (cá)](dictionary.html#e-89)* 'to be ashamed (of)', *[bin' (at)](dictionary.html#e-111)* 'to learn (from)', *[bin' (ca)](dictionary.html#e-112)* 'to pull/impel (toward)'. This isn't word-formation in the coinage sense, but it matters for building *new* verb entries correctly: Boruca verbs are conventionally documented together with their governed postposition, and any new verb coinage should specify this from the outset rather than leaving argument structure implicit.

## 5. Minor processes: rare rather than absent (corrected 2026)

This section previously reported reduplication and prefixation as entirely unattested in Boruca. Rojas Chaves (1992) — a dedicated derivational-morphology study not available to this document's earlier passes — documents small, closed sets of both, so the finding is revised from "not found" to "rare and non-productive, but real." Full detail in `boruca_rojas_chaves_1992_synthesis.md` §4–5.

- **Reduplication — revised.** A handful of attested cases, mostly total reduplication of an athematic monomorphemic root: *cuaj-* → *[cuajcuáj](dictionary.html#e-2779)* 'mariposa' (butterfly), *dó'-* → *dó'dó'* 'rápido' (fast), *dú'-* → *dú'dú'* 'mareado' (dizzy), plus a few "defective theme" forms used only as compounding bases (e.g. *có'có'-*, feeding *có'có'yrá* 'zacate de sabana'). The author explicitly frames this as an infrequent, non-productive pattern, not a systematic one — so for neologism purposes it remains true that reduplication is *not* a safe default coinage strategy, but it is no longer accurate to say it never occurs in the documented language.
- **Prefixation — revised.** Exactly two prefixes are attested, both closed/non-productive: **du-**, forming third-generation kinship nouns (*[só'](dictionary.html#e-3847)* 'abuela' → *[dusó'](dictionary.html#e-3219)* 'bisabuela'), and **quibí-**, forming '[N] days ago' temporal adverbs on numeral bases (*[bú'c](dictionary.html#e-2384)* 'dos' → *quibibú'c* 'anteayer'). The cross-Chibchan comparative paper's point (reference grammar §7) that *Cuna* uses prefixation for subject person-marking while Boruca does not remains valid for **inflectional** morphology — inflection is still suffixation-only in every source reviewed — but the categorical "Boruca has no prefixation" claim for **derivational** morphology was too strong. For a neologism method the practical guidance is unchanged: prefixation is not a productive coinage tool, but two lexicalized-and-closed prefix categories exist and should not be reinvented or extended to new bases.
- **Infixation.** No evidence in any source, including Rojas Chaves (1992) — this negative finding stands unrevised.
- **Blending/clipping** (e.g. portmanteau-style word-shortening as in English "smog"). No clear cases in any source, including Rojas Chaves (1992); Boruca's strategy for complex concepts is compounding full roots or building possessive phrases (§2.8), not blending partial ones.

## 6. Maroto Rojas's own neologism practice (1999): a named speaker's word-formation in action

Everything in §1–§5 above is *inferred* — patterns reconstructed by mining a headword list compiled by many hands over many decades. Espíritu Santo Maroto Rojas's 1999 book (*Lengua o dialecto [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474) o brúnkajk*, recovered and processed 2026; full synthesis in `boruca_maroto_rojas_synthesis.md` §4 and §10) is different in kind: it is a **named, dateable speaker's own account of how he personally coined and adapted vocabulary**, worked examples included, plus ~23,500 words of his own radio-script translations (the "Artículos periodísticos" corpus, §10 of the synthesis doc) that put those strategies into practice at scale on genuinely modern topics (comets, batteries, gunpowder, deep-sea diving, whales, the atmosphere). This is the single most concrete piece of evidence in the whole project for how word-formation actually happened in a living speaker's hands, rather than what the lexicon's surface patterns merely suggest, and it both confirms and extends the compounding/suffixation picture built above.

### 6.1 Semantic extension: reusing an existing root under a new sense

Maroto's presentación documents extending an existing root's meaning to cover a new concept, without any morphological change to the root at all — the cheapest possible coinage strategy, and one entirely invisible to the headword-mining approach of §1–§5 (since the output is not a new *form*, just a new *sense* attached to an old one):

| Palabra base | Significado general | Nuevo sentido |
|---|---|---|
| yuac | decir ('to say') | culpar, sentenciar ('to blame, sentence') |
| brá'shi | guardado ('stored, kept') | prisionero ('prisoner') |
| cahut'cra | cuarto, habitación, lugar ('room, place') | república ('republic') |
| crújc | agarrar, asir ('to grab, seize') | aprender ('to learn') |
| xa'ón | pesado ('heavy') | caro ('expensive') |
| teguá | contado, narrado ('told, narrated') | misterio ('mystery') |
| xán | clavar ('to nail') | someter ('to subdue, subject') |
| yáj | vencer, engañar ('to defeat, deceive') | comprobar ('to verify, prove') |
| shán | contar, relatar ('to count, relate') | estudiar ('to study') |
| shret | raspar ('to scrape') | grabar ('to record') |

The semantic logic is generally metaphorical/associative rather than random: 'to grab/seize' → 'to learn' (grasping an idea), 'to scrape' → 'to record' (of course, an apt image for both incising a surface and — as used throughout the Artículos periodísticos radio scripts — for sound/photographic recording), 'told/narrated' → 'mystery' (something recounted but not fully explained), 'heavy' → 'expensive' (a burden metaphor also common cross-linguistically, including in Spanish and English). This gives a repeatable *method*, not just a list: when coining a term for a new abstract or modern concept, first ask whether an existing concrete root already carries a workable metaphorical extension, before reaching for compounding or borrowing.

### 6.2 Derivation and composition: building a new form from an existing root

Where §2's classifier-compounding evidence shows *[head noun] + [specifier]* compounds built from independently-attested roots, Maroto's own derivational examples show the complementary process — taking a root and extending or combining it into a new derived form, sometimes recognizably suffixal, sometimes a tighter compound than the classifier pattern:

| Palabra base | Significado general | Derivado | Nuevo sentido |
|---|---|---|---|
| huí | saber ('to know') | huíí | científico ('scientist') |
| decyú'cra | correr ('to run') | decyú'cra | corriente ('current [electrical/water]') |
| sú'c | zambullirse ('to dive') | yá'sú'guí | buzo ('diver') |
| chiguidójc | pensar ('to think') | chiguidójigua | inteligencia ('intelligence') |
| bójc | joven ('young') | bojgué | sólido ('solid') |
| dú | ñame ('yam') | dú xít | papa ('potato') |
| o'c | medir ('to measure') | o'guá | medida, acuerdo ('measure; agreement') |
| sót | costumbre, hábito ('custom, habit') | sodrán | tradicional ('traditional') |
| morén | bueno, bien ('good, well') | moréncra | amistad ('friendship') |
| uñ | pez ('fish') | uñ cri'é | ballena ('whale') |
| huic | espíritu ('spirit') | huic chíjgua | cámara fotográfica ('camera') |

Two things stand out for the neologism framework. First, *dú xít* 'potato' (literally 'yam' + *xít*) and *[uñ](dictionary.html?q=u%C3%B1) cri'é* 'whale' (literally 'fish' + specifier) are both **classifier-style compounds built on the same [generic-category head] + [specifier] template already documented in §2** — direct confirmation, from an independent speaker-authored source, that this is a live, general-purpose coinage strategy and not an artifact of how the vocabulary database happened to get compiled. (*[Un cri'é](dictionary.html#e-4663)* 'ballena' and its plural *[un cri'é rojc](dictionary.html#e-4664)* 'cetáceos' also appear as their own headwords in the vocabulary database, source_id=11 — see the whales article in the Artículos periodísticos corpus.) Second, *[huic chíjgua](dictionary.html#e-3327)* 'camera' (literally 'spirit' + a *-uá*-type nominalizer, cf. *[huic](dictionary.html?q=huic) chijguá* already cited in §3 from the grammar papers) independently corroborates the **-uá instrument/machine nominalizer** identified in §3 as the strongest suffixal strategy for naming devices — now attested from two separate source lineages converging on the same word for the same object, about as strong as corroboration gets in a corpus this size. *Huíí* 'científico' and *chiguidójigua* 'inteligencia' show the same nominalizing logic extended to naming people and abstract qualities defined by mental activity, not just physical devices.

### 6.3 Calquing and selective borrowing: strategies documented but not (yet) recommended as models

Maroto's own practice also shows two further strategies, both worth recording for completeness but neither as a positive template the way §6.1–§6.2 are:

- **Syntactic calquing.** Spanish idioms are sometimes translated element-by-element rather than re-expressed natively: *[déc](dictionary.html#e-3078) t'k tá* → 'de vez en cuando' ('once in a while'), *tabáncriñ suátta* → 'dio en el blanco' ('hit the mark'). This produces usable Boruca phrases but imports Spanish phraseology wholesale rather than drawing on native root-and-compound resources — useful to know Maroto did it, but not a strategy to actively teach as a *first* choice for new coinages, per the same caution the reference grammar (§8, gap 8) already raises about Spanish syntactic encroachment more generally. Free-variation doublets in Maroto's own usage — *solo*/*[reshí](dictionary.html?q=resh%C3%AD)*, *científico*/*[huií](dictionary.html#e-3329)* — show this blending happening even within a single speaker's own idiolect, not just across generations.
- **Domain-conditioned wholesale borrowing.** The Artículos periodísticos corpus (§10 of the synthesis doc) shows that for specifically modern technical/scientific vocabulary — the kind with no obvious semantic-extension or compounding target (chemical names, precise scientific units, etc.) — Maroto's actual practice was to **borrow the Spanish term outright**, attaching only native Boruca plural or case morphology rather than coining a native equivalent. This is a real, attested finding about which domains resist internal coinage even for a fluent, linguistically resourceful native speaker actively translating technical material — and it argues that a neologism framework should not treat "always coin natively" as a universal rule, but should expect (and permit) borrowing specifically in the highest-technicality domains, reserving compounding/extension for everyday and moderately technical concepts where Maroto's own practice shows they worked.

### 6.4 Why this matters for §7's ranked strategy list

Maroto's material does not overturn the picture built from the vocabulary database in §1–§5; it **independently confirms the two strongest patterns already identified there** (classifier compounding, the *-uá* nominalizer) using a named speaker's own worked examples rather than inferred structure, and it **adds two genuinely new tools** not otherwise visible in a headword list: semantic extension of an existing root (no new form at all), and an explicit, evidence-based answer to *when borrowing [is](dictionary.html?q=is) the right call* rather than a failure of native coinage. Both are folded into the ranked list in §7 below.

## 7. What this means for a future neologism framework

*This ranked list has now been turned into an actual step-by-step methodology — see `boruca_neologism_framework.md`, which also adds two further strategies developed separately: narrow-scope proto-Chibchan reconstruction and cross-Chibchan adoption/borrowing from sister languages (Cabécar, Bribri), for filling documented gaps and modern-concept coinage respectively.*

This section summarizes the generative rules this analysis actually supports, ranked by evidential strength — the neologism framework builds on these in order, rather than starting from the single *-uá* example that originally motivated this line of work.

1. **Compounding with a semantic-classifier head noun is the strongest, best-attested strategy**, and has direct historical precedent for handling genuinely new domains (turi/cattle for the post-contact livestock domain). New technology concepts are good candidates for this pattern if a suitable classifier head can be identified or established (e.g., a general 'tool/device' or 'machine' head noun, if one exists in the fuller dictionary beyond what's in this database — worth checking Quesada Pacheco & Rojas's full 1999 dictionary if accessible).
2. **The rán'cra 'field of —' pattern is a fully regular, closed rule** and a good template for demonstrating how transparent Boruca's compounding logic can be to learners.
3. **The -uá instrument/machine-nominalizer suffix is productive and specifically suited to naming devices and technology**, with now *three* independent historical precedents (camera/refrigerator/coffee-maker from the grammar papers; the cotton-skein machine from the vocabulary database; Maroto's own *[huic chíjgua](dictionary.html#e-3327)* 'camera', §6.2).
4. **Semantic extension of an existing root is the cheapest and best-attested strategy for new abstract or non-physical concepts** (§6.1) — before compounding or borrowing, check whether an existing concrete root already has a workable metaphorical path to the new sense, the way Maroto's own *[crújc](dictionary.html#e-2750)* 'to grab' → 'to learn' or *[shret](dictionary.html?q=shret)* 'to scrape' → 'to record' do.
5. **New verbs should be causativized analytically** (via *sodíj*/*ón̈*/*shún̈*), and documented together with their governed postposition from the start.
6. **-át is the safe default for new descriptive adjectives**; *-ón/* and *-rani* should be used cautiously or avoided for new coinages, since both show signs of reduced or contested productivity even among the last fluent-ish speakers (reference grammar §8, gap 5, now reinforced by *-rani*'s complete absence from the community dictionary).
7. **A transparent possessive phrase ("[part] of [whole]") is a legitimate alternative to a fused compound** (§2.8, Rojas Chaves 1992) — for concepts that don't cleanly fit an existing classifier head, a new coinage doesn't have to be a single word; Boruca's own anatomy vocabulary shows the syntactic route was, if anything, preferred over morphological fusion for this domain.
8. **Do not rely on reduplication or prefixation as productive coinage tools** — both exist in the documented language (2026 correction, §5) but only as small, closed, non-productive sets (kinship terms, day-counting adverbs, a handful of onomatopoeic-feeling animal/motion words), not as generative rules a new coinage could extend. Infixation and blending remain entirely unattested.
9. **Reserve outright Spanish borrowing for the highest-technicality domains**, following Maroto's own documented practice (§6.3) — compounding, the *-uá* nominalizer, and semantic extension should be tried first for everyday and moderately technical concepts, with borrowing (native morphology attached) as the deliberate fallback for concepts (precise scientific/technical terms) that resisted native coinage even for a fluent native speaker.
10. **Distinguish deep-inherited devices from recent, terminal-period innovations when presenting coinage rules to learners, and don't be afraid to make systematic choices the historical record itself was already heading toward (2026 addition, §1.5)**. Classifier-compounding and *-crá*/*-cua* suffixation trace to protochibchan material roughly 4,600–5,400+ years old (Constenla 2008, 2011) — using them for new coinages continues a word-formation lineage far older than Boruca's entire documented contact history with Spanish, which is worth stating plainly in teaching materials as a source of legitimacy and pride, not just historical trivia. Separately, Boruca's grammar was demonstrably still changing on its own terms in its final documented century — the tense-to-aspect reorganization of *-ra/-irá/-krá/-kirá* and the *ang*-focus-marker fusion into incipient person-marking morphology (Castro, Murillo & Quesada 2009/2011; full detail in `boruca_protochibchan_development_synthesis.md`) were both caught mid-transition by the textual record, not merely reconstructed. This gives real precedent for a modernization program to make deliberate systematic choices — e.g., teaching the aspectual analysis of the TAM system as primary, since that is where the documented language was already heading — rather than presenting every historically-attested form as equally fixed and equally authoritative.

Open item before finalizing a coinage methodology: it would be worth checking whether the fuller Quesada Pacheco & Rojas (1999) dictionary (cited throughout the grammar papers but not itself among the files reviewed for this project) contains additional classifier-head nouns beyond *[các](dictionary.html?q=c%C3%A1c)*, *dí'*, *[turi](dictionary.html?q=turi)*, *[ca'ísh](dictionary.html?q=ca%27%C3%ADsh)*, *rán'cra*, and the smaller *sa-* 'cabeza' set (§2.8) — the six found here are almost certainly not exhaustive, just the ones with enough entries in this particular 2,255-word sample (plus Rojas Chaves 1992's independent example set) to be statistically visible. Rojas Chaves (1992) §Appendix also documents a small, fully regular locational-adverb paradigm (six forms, crossing "toward/away," "up/down," and "1st/2nd plane" — see `boruca_rojas_chaves_1992_synthesis.md` §9) that, while not itself a template for *new* coinages, is a good illustration for teaching materials of how systematic Boruca's productive morphology can be even at small scale.

# Historical Periods of Boruca

Periodizing the sociolinguistic history of Boruca (Brunka) matters for revival work because each stage left a different kind of evidence — raw wordlists, grammatical sketches, terminal-speaker recordings, or only secondary memory — and each stage explains *why* the language changed the way it did (contact intensity, schooling policy, community attitude). Knowing which period a source belongs to tells revivalists how reliable, how complete, and how "authentic" (pre- vs. post-attrition) that source's forms are. The periodization below is built primarily from Quesada Pacheco (1994), the richest source on the 19th century, supplemented by Castro (2008) for demographic/20th-21st century detail and J. Diego Quesada (2000) for the terminal-speaker/language-death mechanics.

## Period 1: Pre-documentation / Early Colonial Contact (pre-1862)

Boruca is first attested only indirectly: general documentation of Costa Rican indigenous languages begins in the late 18th century (Fernández 1892, *Lenguas indígenas de Centro América en el siglo XVIII*), but no Boruca-specific manuscript survives from before 1862. The Borucas had reportedly been bilingual in Spanish "since the colonial period" (J. Diego Quesada 2000, fn. 6), reflecting early, comparatively cooperative contact with the Spanish — the Boruca are remembered as early allies of the Spaniards, in contrast to other indigenous groups who resisted, which neighboring groups still cite pejoratively as *vividores* ("hustlers") (Fernández 1886, cited in Quesada 2000). The Fiesta de los Diablitos (masked mock-battles against "the Spanish," still performed every December 31) preserves a folk memory of open conflict predating this accommodation (Castro 2008). No linguistic data exists from this period; it is reconstructed only from ethnohistorical framing in the later sources.

**The founding and consolidation of the pueblo of Boruca (2026 addition).** Boza Villarreal (2005, "Población indígena de la Gran Talamanca, 1840-1927"; full synthesis in `boruca_boza_villarreal_2005_synthesis.md`) adds precise historical-demographic detail to this period. The pueblo of Boruca was founded permanently by Franciscan missionaries roughly a decade before neighboring Térraba, initially with people of the Boruca *cacicazgo* specifically; over the course of the 18th century the abubaes, cotos, and quepos peoples were also relocated into the same pueblo — the Quepo relocation specifically is now precisely dated: per Quesada Pacheco (1996, "El idioma de los quepos"; full synthesis in `boruca_tangential_chibchan_papers_synthesis.md`), citing an official 1749 report by governor Luis Díez Navarro, the nearly-extinct Quepo pueblo (collapsed to 3 households by then, from 55 people in 1708) was formally gathered into Boruca **in 1748 by fray Juan Montoya, the Franciscan guardián of the Boruca convent** — sharpening the "1749" date already in this document's Summary Timeline below, which reflects the report's date rather than the event's own. Whether Quepo was a distinct language, a Boruca dialect, or effectively the same language remains genuinely unresolved: Quesada Pacheco (1996) — working only from ~70 unglossed Quepo personal names in colonial records, no common vocabulary — treats a close Quepo-Boruca relationship as plausible but stops short of claiming identity, and his own phonological-feature comparison (Quepo shares more features with Huetar than with Boruca) if anything complicates a tight link; a later, independently-authored source (Constenla Umaña & Ibarra Rojas 2009, working from archival/ethnohistorical territorial mapping) is more confident, stating the Quepo and Coto peoples "probablemente hablaban boruca." This project should present the relationship as probable but unproven, citing both. Although these constituent groups were initially distinguished by origin, by the late 1700s the term "boruca" had generalized to all inhabitants of the pueblo and its environs regardless of ancestral group — a precise dating (roughly a century earlier than this project's other evidence) for the ethnonym-generalization process already discussed via Constenla (1977) in the reference grammar's kinship section (§9). The source also records a second etymology for "Boruca/Brunca" worth setting alongside the *bruñ* 'ceniza' + *caje* 'zanja' analysis already in this project's materials: citing Doris Stone (1949:4), it glosses *Brun* as 'cenizas' (ashes) and *ka* as 'adentro' (inside) — agreeing on the "ash" element but diverging on the second, an unresolved etymological detail rather than a settled one.

**A third etymology, and two new named 16th-century caciques (2026 addition).** Two further survey papers (Quesada Pacheco 1999-2000 and Constenla Umaña 2011; full syntheses in `boruca_cr_indigenous_languages_surveys_synthesis.md`) add a **third, again not fully reconcilable, etymology**: *Turucaca* (an early name/place tied to Boruca history) is derived from *Burucaca* (< boruca */brúŋkahk/*), itself analyzed as boruca */kahk/* 'oquedad' (hollow/cavity) — agreeing with neither the "zanja" (ditch) nor the "adentro" (inside) analyses of the second element already on record, so this project now has three independent, mutually inconsistent scholarly etymologies for its own name and should treat the question as genuinely open rather than resolved. The same two sources also name two 16th-century caciques not previously documented here: **Giriara/Xiriara** (spelling varies by source; likely the same figure), who governed both Borucaca (now Boruca) and Turucaca, and **Guayaví**, cacique of Boruca specifically. Constenla (2011) additionally names two "suquias o chamanes" appearing in Spanish-language community narration alongside the already-well-documented Cuasrán, Sancrahua, and the Mamrán: **Seredrún** and **Suatuj** — new figures for this project's roster of named legendary/historical Boruca persons, though neither source gives any narrative detail about them beyond the bare names.

**The Diablitos festival's own cosmology: Tatica Kuasran and Sancrawa.** Castro (2008), citing a 2007 web source ("Kan Tan Project, Costa Rica"), describes the Fiesta de los Diablitos as a celebration of belief "in the mythological god **Tatica Kuasran** and his son **Sancrawa**" — her own transliteration of the figures this project's other sources call **Cuasrán** and **Sancráhua/Sankráua**. This is now the **third independent attestation** of the same father-son pairing, joining Quesada Pacheco (2006)'s toponymy entry for the place name *[Cuasrán](dictionary.html#e-4461)* and Maroto Rojas's own recorded legend "La historia de Kuasrán" (in *Lengua o dialecto [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474) o brúnkajk*, 1999; see `boruca_maroto_rojas_synthesis.md` §6), which likewise names Kuasrán's son as Sankráua and describes him as having "una historia todavía más largo y bonito" ("an even longer and more beautiful story") than his father's — a story that, as far as this project's sources go, has still never actually been recorded on its own. *Tatica* is a Spanish affectionate/honorific term (roughly "dear father/grandfather"); Castro is also the first source in this project to call Cuasrán a "god" outright rather than a "legendary being" or "spiritual leader." Castro flags an unresolved tension worth carrying forward rather than smoothing over: elsewhere in the same paper she states "there is no evidence of original Boruca religious practices" and that the oldest semi-speakers she interviewed describe themselves as having "always been Catholic" — the Tatica Kuasran material is evidently treated by the community itself as festival tradition, bracketed apart from "religion" in a way her paper doesn't fully reconcile.

**A fourth, and by far the most detailed, independent source: Rojas Chaves (1988) on the festival itself (2026 addition).** Carmen Rojas Chaves's "Descripción y análisis de la fiesta de los diablitos de Boruca" (*Estudios de Lingüística Chibcha* 7, 1988), based on fieldwork including direct observation of the December 1986 celebration, is the single richest ethnographic description of the Diablitos festival in this project's corpus, using Dell Hymes's (1962) ethnography-of-speaking framework. The festival runs four days (30–31 December, 1–2 January) and is structured as a dramatized battle between indios and españoles, ending in indigenous victory — "y éste es el mensaje que quiere dar la festividad." Key roles: a "diablo mayor" (senior devil, a community-leader figure — the role was held for many years by **don Espíritu Santo Maroto** himself, and passed to a family member after his death, suggesting the position may be informally hereditary), two guardians who supervise the diablitos' conduct, and "el toro" (the bull, representing the Spanish, played by a rotating community member inside a wooden-and-cloth-and-mask armature). Participation as a diablito requires being male, an adult but younger than the diablo mayor/guardians, and Boruca or related to Borucas by kinship. Two formal speech events — requesting permission to tie the bull to a tree (31 Dec.) and to release it (1 Jan.) — were still performed partly in Boruca as of Rojas Chaves's fieldwork, code-switching between a boruca-language ceremonial core and Spanish elaboration; she transcribes both in full with interlinear glossing (see below). A third, informal speech event closes the festival on 2 January: a mock "sale" of the slaughtered bull's meat that turns into a ritualized exchange of insults among the men present, in Spanish with occasional Boruca profanity — Rojas Chaves notes this is now "lo único que los participantes saben decir en esta lengua" for some speakers, i.e., the insults are the only Boruca some participants retain active command of.

**A new, previously undocumented piece of Tatica Kuasrán folklore.** Rojas Chaves records a specific taboo tied directly to the festival's cosmology, not present in any of this project's other Cuasrán/Tatica Kuasran sources: during the festival days it is forbidden to leave the village or climb the surrounding hills, especially **Cerro Volcán**. Whoever does so risks being "lost" by **Tatica Cuasrán** himself, or — if permitted to return — being sent bad luck and assorted punishments over the following year, with the severity increasing specifically for Cerro Volcán. This is a genuinely new data point for Cuasrán's role as an active, still-feared supernatural authority (rather than only a culture-hero/legendary figure), and is worth reading alongside the existing Tatica Kuasran material above.

**Corroborating/extending prior population and vitality figures.** Rojas Chaves cites Bozzoli (1986) for a Boruca population of 1,174, of which ~72% were 30 or younger and 51.8%/48.2% male/female — a population figure that should be read alongside, and does not fully reconcile with, other population counts elsewhere in this document (a reminder that 20th-century Boruca population figures vary by source and methodology and haven't been harmonized in this project). On language vitality, Bozzoli's count is more pessimistic ("cinco personas que hablan boruca y once personas que entienden esta lengua" as of 1986), but Rojas Chaves's own fieldwork gives a higher figure: "diez hablantes borucas, tres de los cuales viven fuera de Boruca; y hay, por lo menos, treinta personas en Boruca que hablan un poco de este idioma." This "ten fluent speakers" figure is worth flagging as a probable match — not just a coincidence — with the "exactly ten fluent Boruca speakers as of 1992, all over 50 and six of them over 75" figure already cited above from Rojas Chaves (1992:37) via Constenla: since both figures come from the same researcher only four years apart, this project cannot rule out that the 1992 citation is restating rather than independently reconfirming the 1988 count, and any future synthesis should treat "ten fluent speakers, late 1980s–early 1990s" as a single data point rather than two independent ones until the underlying fieldwork can be checked.

**Loss of the Boruca-language ceremonial script.** Rojas Chaves records that not many years before her fieldwork, the entire festival was conducted in Boruca, because **don Espíritu Santo Maroto had written out all the ceremonies in Boruca** and participants memorized them. Following his death and the loss of his manuscripts, portions were dropped — some through simple forgetting, others because remaining participants could no longer render them in Boruca at all — leaving only fragments in community memory by 1988. This directly corroborates and dates one specific mechanism (not just general attrition, but the loss of a specific written ceremonial text after its author's death) behind the broader pattern of Boruca-language loss in ritual contexts documented elsewhere in this project via Maroto's own surviving manuscripts (`boruca_maroto_rojas_synthesis.md`).

**Two short bilingual ceremonial texts, not yet added to `boruca_vocabulary.db`.** Rojas Chaves transcribes, with interlinear glossing, the "as currently practiced" Boruca-language core of both permission-speech events (tying and untying the bull) — short exchanges between the diablo mayor and a household head, in which the mayor greets the householder, explains (mixing Boruca and Spanish) that the bull is a Spaniard who damages crops and homes, and offers exaggerated payment for any harm the bull does, tied symbolically with a golden chain "en referencia al oro que los borucas tenían en el pasado" (recalling the gold the Borucas once had). These are short but genuine, datable (1986–88) primary-source ceremonial Boruca, distinct in register and function from the narrative/song material already in this project's corpus. As with the Constenla (1986) narratives (see `boruca_three_sources_synthesis.md`), the plain-text extraction of these two passages is noisy enough (broken diacritics, inconsistent line breaks) that vocabulary should be pulled from a page-image re-transcription rather than this pass — flagged as follow-up work rather than inserted into the DB now.

## Period 2: First Foreign Documentation / "The 19th-Century Manuscripts" (1862–1896)

This is the best-documented period thanks to Quesada Pacheco (1994). Four foreign amateur investigators, none linguistically trained, recorded Boruca in succession, each improving on the last: **J.J. Valentini (1862)** produced the first known Boruca vocabulary, 147 entries (manuscript today at the Van Pelt–Dietrich Library, University of Pennsylvania, likely a copy in C.H. Berendt's hand); **William Gabb (1875, published 1875/1882 in Fernández's *Colección de documentos*)** compiled a 250-entry Boruca-Spanish vocabulary and is the first to explicitly document tone ("accent") in a related language (bribri) and the first to record language shift in progress; **Bernardo Augusto Thiel (1882)** produced by far the largest 19th-century vocabulary (1,100 entries), the first to attempt phrases and conjugated verbs and to record toponyms, plus a separate unpublished 93-entry glossary (held at the Archivo Eclesiástico de la Curia Metropolitana) that shows interference from Térraba, suggesting a bilingual informant; and **Henri Pittier**, who did fieldwork 1892–1896 (published only in 1941) and produced the richest 19th-century corpus — 1,250 lexical entries, toponyms, grammatical example sentences, and three bilingual texts plus two Spanish-only traditional narrations, including a narrative dictated by informant Jesús Sánchez about an 1896 expedition (13–31 March) to the Burica peninsula. Pittier candidly admitted he never mastered spoken Boruca beyond daily-use dialogue (Pittier 1941:7). Crucially, Gabb in 1875 already reported the first documented sign of language attrition: "the Borucas are rapidly acquiring Spanish… in a group of five Borucas, not one could count except in Spanish" (Gabb 1875/1886:405) — showing shift to Spanish was underway at least a generation before compulsory schooling. All four investigators were foreigners (German, French, English/American mother tongues); Quesada Pacheco (1994) notes the near-total absence of Costa Rican national interest in the language during this period. Phonetic/graphic analysis of these manuscripts shows the glottal stop, tone marking, and the /pʃ/~/kʃ/ to /ʃ/ merger were already unstable or in transition by the 1890s, evidence of a language already undergoing internal change, possibly accelerated by contact.

## Period 3: Early Scientific and Ethnographic Interest (1900s–1949)

Foreign scholarly attention continued into the early 20th century even as sociopolitical pressure on the language intensified domestically. Walter Lehmann produced a Boruca-language manuscript ("Boruca Sprache," 1908, Ibero-American Institute, Berlin) and an unpublished travel diary (*Tagebuch Costa Rica*), later synthesizing Central American languages in *Zentral-Amerika* (1920). Elías Leiva gave a 1908 conference on a journey through the General, Térraba, and Boruca regions (published in *Páginas Ilustradas*). Before his 1941 book, Pittier had already published two Brunka-attributed legends in "Folk-Lore of the Bribri and Brunka Indians in Costa Rica" (*Journal of American Folklore* 16, 1903; not on the DIPALICORI list, acquired opportunistically — 2026 addition): "The King of the Wild Hogs" (a supernatural hog-king near "San-kra-ua" who punishes a Boruca hunter for wounding but not killing his animals, then teaches him to hunt properly) and "Don Pedro Cascante" (the origin legend of the deep hollow trail of El Pito, in which a missionary permanently binds a shape-shifting "Encanto" — later revealed to be the Devil — that had been exacting a human toll from travelers on the ridge; a later, avaricious rancher, Cascante, strikes a bargain with the same Encanto for gold, prospers, but is ultimately taken by it at death). Neither legend is Boruca-language material (both are recorded in Spanish/English only), but the "Encanto" figure — a shape-shifting, deal-making supernatural being tied to a specific mountain trail — is worth setting alongside this project's existing Tatica Kuasrán material (Period 1) as a second, independently documented type of localized Boruca-territory supernatural figure, distinct from but structurally comparable to Cuasrán's own mountain-based enchantment. Pittier's 1892–96 field materials proper were finally published posthumously-in-effect in 1941 by the Museo Nacional. Domestically, this period is identified by J. Diego Quesada (2000) as the true turning point for the language's decline: "the universalization of compulsory and free elementary education at the turn of the century" brought non-Indian (*si'kwa*) teachers into remote Boruca areas who prohibited use of the language in and out of class and physically punished children for speaking it (elderly informants in the 1990s still bore scars from being struck with textbook spines). Quesada Pacheco (1995:13) notes that despite this, a few monolingual Boruca speakers still existed even during the first half of the 20th century.

**Doris Stone's 1945–46 fieldwork** (published 1949 as *The [Boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474) of Costa Rica*, Peabody Museum; consulted here in the 2013 Spanish translation, *Los Borucas de Costa Rica*, tr. María Eugenia Bozzoli Vargas) is this period's richest primary source and gives the project's first independent, dated snapshot of the language's status in the gap between Pittier (1892–96) and the 1970s MEP revival. Stone counted **641 Boruca people total across five settlements** as of 1945: the main village of Boruca (47 houses, 334 inhabitants), Palmar Norte (117, increasingly non-indigenous due to United Fruit Co. proximity), Curré (103, 14 houses), Lagarto (15, 3 houses), plus smaller populations at Maíz, Puerto Cortés, and Buenos Aires and roughly 27 people living dispersed in the forest along the Río Chánguena. This is a marked drop from the **350–400 inhabitants and 60–65 houses** Pittier counted in the main village alone in 1891 (per an appended excerpt of Pittier's own 1891 travel account included in the same translated volume) — consistent with either real population decline, a shrinking core village as people dispersed to the other four settlements, or differing census methods across the 54-year gap; the sources don't allow distinguishing between these. Stone is explicit that the Boruca people of her fieldwork are themselves a merged, composite group — descendants of the Coto, Turucaca, Burucac, Quepo, and Abubaes peoples consolidated under Spanish colonial reducción policy (a process traceable in colonial records from "Burucac" first appearing in 1563 through the 1666 "reducción de Nuestra Señora de la Concepción de Boruca" and an 1749 resettlement that added Quepo people to the same town) — and she treats the contemporary language itself the same way: *"El idioma actual es una mezcla del habla de varias gentes integrada en los modernos borucas"* ('the current language is a mixture of the speech of several peoples integrated into modern Borucas'), while noting linguists nonetheless classified it as Chibchan even in this heterogeneous state (Stone, citing Mason 1940; cf. Lehmann 1920, vol. II, placing it near Cueva-Cuna in the western Chibchan branch).

Stone also documents a **school-based Boruca-language teaching program already active by 1945** — a full three decades before the MEP revitalization efforts usually cited as the starting point for institutional language revival (see Period 4). Under the newly founded **Junta de Protección de las Razas Aborígenes de la Nación** (est. 6 December 1945, with Stone herself as founding president), local schools had assigned Boruca-language teachers and a cotton-weaving teacher, and Stone reports children were "filling a notebook with the help of the older generation" and being encouraged to collect legends and study their own language — a practice she describes as *"inaudito hace unos pocos años"* ('unheard of a few years earlier'). This appears to be a distinct, earlier, and until now undocumented episode of institutional language revitalization in this project's sources, rather than a precursor folded into the 1970s MEP narrative. Whether it had lasting effect or lapsed before the 1970s effort began is not addressed by Stone's short-visit fieldwork (she is candid that her account rests on only four brief visits, three of them tied to Inter-American Highway impact assessment, not a sustained ethnographic study) and is not yet established by any other source in this project.

Alongside this teaching effort, Stone records concrete evidence of lexical erosion already underway by 1945: informants told her they had **forgotten their own word for the huipil-style garment**, though they affirmed one had existed and now simply used the Spanish term; and the native numeral system was already restricted to **1–8**, with Spanish supplying all higher counting. She also draws an explicit parallel between the difficulty of eliciting consistent legend-narratives and of eliciting language data in the field — *"la narración de leyendas era similar, hasta cierto punto, a la recolección de datos sobre el lenguaje. Pocas personas recordaban detalles y no había dos [que](dictionary.html?q=que) estuvieran de acuerdo en todos ellos"* — an implicit period-appropriate acknowledgment that even 1940s speaker knowledge was already inconsistent, not a stable baseline "pure" state to measure later attrition against.

**A decade-by-decade population trajectory, 1844-1927, and an early, sobering language-shift data point (2026 addition).** Boza Villarreal (2005; full synthesis in `boruca_boza_villarreal_2005_synthesis.md`), working from 19th/early-20th-century travelers' estimates, missionary reports, and original national census manuscripts, gives this project its first quantitative Boruca population figures predating Stone's 1945 count: 362 in 1844 (*El Mentor Costarricense*); Boruca+Térraba combined 644 in the 1864 census; 285-600 (varying by source/method) through the 1880s-1890s, with independent travelers' estimates and the 1883/1892 censuses converging reassuringly on a 330-400 range for Boruca specifically across that period (Térraba 230-300, Cabagra 50-80 — though none of these figures include the Cabécar of Ujarrás, so the true combined Pacific-South indigenous population was likely closer to 1,000 in both years); roughly 350 around 1900 (Krautwig); 509 by a ~1913 estimate; and a combined Térraba+Boruca+El Pozo figure of 741 in a 1916 estimate. Bishop Thiel, Fr. Krautwig, Fr. Mayorga, and Pittier all independently reported the population — Térraba's especially — declining rapidly in this era; Krautwig attributed it to an epidemic locally called "fiebre negra" (black fever, leaving corpses with black bruise-like marks), which he reports hit Térraba harder than Boruca, attributing the difference to Boruca's cooler climate.

Boza Villarreal's original analysis of the **1927 census manuscripts** adds a genuinely new and sobering data point that sits in tension with this document's own Period 4 framing below: of the Pacific-South indigenous population, **92.8% reported Spanish as mother tongue and only ~5% reported an indigenous language — and 98.5% of that 5% were recorded specifically in the Bribri pueblo of Cabagra**, meaning the census recorded almost no one in Boruca or Térraba themselves as having an indigenous mother tongue as early as 1927, decades before the "1950s-1970s shifting generation" this document otherwise identifies (via Castro 2008 and J.D. Quesada 2000) as the decisive turning point. This should be treated as a real and important data point, but not over-read: the source doesn't break the mother-tongue figures out finely enough to confirm the residual ~1.5% weren't concentrated in Boruca rather than Térraba; self-reported "lengua materna" to non-indigenous census enumerators, in the same era Period 3 already documents as one of active school-based language suppression, is a plausible undercount mechanism rather than a clean measure of actual home-language use; and this project's other sources (Constenla & Maroto, Quesada Pacheco, Castro) document fluent elderly speakers still active as late as the 1990s-2000s, which fits a language that was already a reported minority home-language by 1927 but continued to be transmitted to at least some children for several more decades — not one whose transmission had already fully stopped by that date. The 1927 finding is best read as evidence that Spanish's dominance was already substantially further along, and considerably earlier, than this document's existing Period 4 narrative alone would suggest, rather than as a wholesale revision of that narrative's basic shape.

*Source-completeness note (resolved):* Stone's original publication included an "Appendix C: Comparación de Vocabularios," a comparative table drawing on eight sources/columns (Valentini 1862/1920, Gabb 1881, Thiel 1882, Thiel 1920, Pittier 1891, Pittier 1941, Pittier 1908, and Stone's own 1945 fieldwork in IPA transcription). This table did not survive the initial plain-text PDF extraction (it is a dense multi-column layout that linearized as nothing between the phonetic key and the photo-plates section) but has since been recovered by rendering the original pages as images (PDF pages 103–118, printed pages ~101–109) and transcribing them visually. The recovered table — 189 entries spanning sections A through Y, including all footnotes — is now stored as its own table (`stone_appendix_c_1949`) in `boruca_thiel_1882_wordlist.db`, kept separate from the main vocabulary database for the same reason as the Thiel 1882 comparative material: it is for cross-linguistic and etymological analysis, not confirmed modern Boruca vocabulary.

## Period 4: Generational Language Shift and Contraction (1950s–1970s)

This period is sparsely documented directly (no dedicated period sources survive; it is reconstructed from retrospective testimony in Castro 2008 and J. Diego Quesada 2000) — **with one direct exception (2026 addition)**: Adolfo Constenla Umaña's 1975 kinship-terminology fieldwork, published as "La terminología de parentesco de los indios borucas" (1977; full synthesis in `boruca_constenla_1977_kinship_synthesis.md`), which lands squarely inside this period and is this project's only genuine contemporaneous linguistic-fieldwork snapshot of it. Constenla worked from one informant's own genealogy, cross-checked against two further genealogies collected on a follow-up visit, drawing exclusively on informants aged 60 or older — meaning his data reflects the kinship knowledge of people born around or before 1915, a generation older than the "shifting generation" described below. Two cautionary incest narratives were recorded: one about two siblings whose relationship, discovered by the *suquias* (shamans), leads to their being devoured (the woman by tigers, the man by a cayman); another about a father (himself a suquia) and his daughter, who dies in childbirth, after which the community's men take the father away to kill him but he escapes. (A minor correction to Doris Stone's 1949 monograph surfaces here: Stone had read the term *Cacyrá*, associated with this same tradition, as the name of a Boruca lineage/group; Constenla clarifies it is actually just the place-name where the woman's punishment occurred.) Constenla also recorded explicit norms governing behavior between relatives — formal, non-joking relations required between cross-sex siblings/cousins; extra respect for same-sex older siblings/cousins; strict formality between cross-sex uncles/aunts and nieces/nephews (and, apparently, between parents- and children-in-law); somewhat less formality between same-sex uncles/aunts and nieces/nephews; deep respect generally due to grandparents and elders, tempered with informality toward young grandchildren specifically — a rare direct window into social norms of this generation, not available from any other source in this project. He also records a small, concrete piece of attrition evidence predating the "shifting generation" framing below: a second, more precise term for 'borucas', *brúñshuá [rójc](dictionary.html?q=r%C3%B3jc)* (literally 'people of the refuse/waste'), was already "desusada" (disused) by 1975 in favor of the still-current *[brúñcajc](dictionary.html#e-2328) [rójc](dictionary.html?q=r%C3%B3jc)* — a documented case of lexical loss within the ethnonym system itself, occurring a full generation before the language-shift dynamics described next. The kinship system itself (Hawaiian-type in ego's generation, lineal in the first ascending generation) is covered in the reference grammar's new §9, not here.

The generation that grew up under the repressive school regime of Period 3 — including terminal speaker Don Ernesto González and fluent elder Doña Paulina Leiva — became the "shifting generation": adults who were themselves fluent but, out of fear and internalized shame, raised their own children in Spanish, interrupting normal intergenerational transmission. J. Diego Quesada (2000) characterizes this as "radical death": language shift and death compressed into one or two generations, rather than the slower multi-generational "gradual death" seen elsewhere, meaning the last fluent speakers largely retained full competence (little structural decay) even as the language essentially stopped being passed on. From the 1970s, the Costa Rican Ministry of Public Education (MEP) began indigenous-language revival efforts, but — per prior project research — with limited success in producing new active speakers, consistent with the "damage was already done" assessment in Quesada (2000).

## Period 5: Salvage Linguistics and the Terminal-Speaker Era (1979–2000)

**A documented shift from shame to pride, and a named 1989 UCR outreach project (2026 addition).** Quesada Pacheco (1999-2000, "Situación actual y futuro de las lenguas indígenas de Costa Rica"; full synthesis in `boruca_cr_indigenous_languages_surveys_synthesis.md`) reports that "la vergüenza de que hablaba el maestro Maroto en los años 70 se ha convertido en orgullo entre los borucas" — the shame Espíritu Santo Maroto spoke of in the 1970s had, by the time of writing (1999-2000), turned into pride among Borucas — a documented positive attitudinal shift roughly a decade before this document's own Period 7 dates the start of the "digital revival movement," worth treating as an early, pre-digital precursor to that later, more visible phase. The same paper independently names and dates a specific project behind the dictionary-informant activity already documented below for 1989-1990: **"Difusión de la lengua boruca,"** led by Carmen Rojas (the same Carmen Rojas Chaves already documented elsewhere in this project) under UCR's Vicerrectoría de Acción Social, running from the second semester of 1989, involving regular travel to Boruca communities to train teachers and interview remaining speakers, and preparing both a children's course and (with Quesada Pacheco) a Boruca-Spanish dictionary. A specific earlier literacy resource is also newly documented: Carla Jara's *Abecedario ilustrado [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)* (1986, San José: ANEIN/MEP), part of a multi-language illustrated-primer series. A companion paper by the same Carmen Rojas Chaves (1997-98, "Revitalización lingüística de las lenguas indígenas de Costa Rica"; full synthesis in `boruca_tangential_chibchan_papers_synthesis.md`) adds a precise starting date for a further institutional mechanism: **from 1993, the Ministry of Education began contracting community members as paid indigenous-language teachers, and "la experiencia se inició con un maestro de lengua boruca"** (the program began with a Boruca-language teacher) before expanding to other languages/territories year over year; by the time of writing, 3 of 9 indigenous-language teachers in the Buenos Aires region taught Boruca specifically, part of a national total of 24 teachers covering 60% of indigenous schools. The 1982 Radio Cultural Boruca station, already documented below, turns out to have been part of a broader initiative that also established a cultural radio station in Guatuso; Quesada Pacheco's own contemporary assessment of the Boruca station is unsparing — "al menos la radio de Boruca en la actualidad no se presta en lo absoluto para la difusión de la lengua y cultura borucas" — independently corroborating, from an earlier source, this document's existing "attempt failed" framing (via Castro 2008).

A concentrated new wave of scholarly documentation coincided with the language's final active phase. Adolfo Constenla and native speaker/self-taught linguist Espíritu Santo Maroto published *Leyendas y tradiciones borucas* (1979/1986, Editorial UCR), a grammatical sketch plus bilingual legend corpus, expanded in its second edition with four further narratives Constenla recorded from Paulina Leiva Morales on 13 February 1986.

**The oral-narrative corpus, its own speech-event conventions, and a structuralist reading of its ethics (2026 addition).** Two further sources — Constenla (1986), "Textos bilingües de cuatro narraciones tradicionales borucas" (full text acquired via web-fetch text extraction, OCR-quality caveats noted in `boruca_alvarado_chaves_1989_synthesis.md`), and Magda Lorena Alvarado Chaves (1989), "Las actantes en la narración tradicional boruca" (*Filología y Lingüística* XV(2), a Greimassian structuralist analysis of all 18 narratives in the 1979/1986 collection; full synthesis in the same file) — together give this project its fullest picture yet of Boruca oral narrative as a living genre, not just a fixed text corpus. Constenla documents narration as a **speech event with its own conventions**: told in the kitchen/dining area after the evening meal, always by elders (younger community members who know stories are reluctant to tell them publicly, "porque piensan que su conocimiento de ellas es inferior, lo cual suele ser cierto" — a candid, period-specific admission of felt generational inadequacy), with active audience participation (corrections, added detail, especially from elders present), and delivered in a Boruca/Spanish code-mix whose Boruca proportion was already visibly shrinking by 1986 — fully Boruca-language tellings occurred, even then, only when elders deliberately helped each other recall a story or a researcher specifically requested it. Community narrators themselves draw a **truth-value distinction** between native-origin legends (told as literally true, with tellers pointing to real local landmarks the stories explain — the stream said to have formed when a giant serpent's cave collapsed, footprint-like rock formations attributed to a water spirit's daughters, the mountain where the rebel Cuasrán still lives enchanted) and borrowed Hispanic animal tales like Tío Conejo (not accorded the same truth-status) — a genuinely useful analytical frame for classifying this project's whole narrative corpus going forward.

Alvarado Chaves's structural analysis of the same corpus finds a striking **inversion of European folktale's value polarity**: where Greimas's original (European) actantial model treats the subject, object, dispatcher, receiver, and helper as canonically positive and only the opponent as negative, the great majority of Boruca narratives run the opposite way — a negative subject (a character whose desire threatens or violates community norms) opposed by a typically **positive** opponent (usually the community itself, or a natural/supernatural guardian, acting to stop the transgression). She proposes a single ethical maxim underlying nearly the whole corpus: **"No hay que desear lo que por naturaleza corresponde a otros"** ("one must not desire what by nature belongs to others"), with narratives splitting into rule-violators who meet a tragic, community-protecting end (the majority) versus rule-followers who suffer no misfortune (a minority, e.g. "Historia del abuelo Isabel," where the community collectively and successfully conceals a silver mine from foreigners). Structurally, she also finds the Subject and Dispatcher roles are **never played by different actors** in any of the 18 narratives (total syncretism), and that the Helper role is nearly always absent — both a clear departure from the European folktale structure the model was built on.

Several individual narratives from this pair of sources directly extend or cross-reference material already in this document. The **bróbróyrá** ('little tritons') legend — elderly Borucas historically luring small scaled sea-children with drums and macaw feathers to kill them for bone necklaces, until the sea's guardian spirit (named **úñquí**, glossed "sirena" by the narrator) punishes the practice by draining a young worker's blood — gives full narrative context to the single lexical citation of *[bróbróyrá](dictionary.html#e-2307)* already in `boruca_word_formation.md` §2.1 (via Rojas Chaves 1992). The **Sancrahua** narrative (Alvarado Chaves's summary of the same 1979-collection legend already treated at length via Castro 2008, Quesada Pacheco 2006, Maroto Rojas 1999, and Rojas Chaves 1988 above) adds a specific etymology not previously in this project's materials: **Cerro Pelón**, where Cuasrán takes his son Sancrahua, means "bald/naked mountain." The same collection's **"Historia de la guerra entre los borucas y los extranjeros"** offers an additional etiological account directly tying the Fiesta de los Diablitos to a battle narrative (the diablitos represent Borucas, the ceremonial bull the foreigners) — worth reading alongside, though not necessarily reconciled with, the fuller ethnographic festival material already given above via Rojas Chaves (1988). And the **Historia de Johnson** — a foreigner who settles in Boruca, becomes a police official, and (per Alvarado Chaves's fuller summary) later attempts and fails to steal Cuasrán's treasure — turns out to be the **same narrative J.D. Quesada excerpts as a worked grammatical example** in his 1996 topicalization study (`boruca_quesada_1996_anj_synthesis.md` §6, example 21), which quotes only the story's peaceful opening; the two sources together now give a fuller picture of the same text than either alone, a small but pleasing case of this project's cross-source triangulation paying off. No vocabulary from either source was added to `boruca_vocabulary.db` in this pass, consistent with the OCR-quality caution already applied to other narrative sources in this project — new terms surfaced here (*úñquí*, the shaman-figure *bóvs*, trade goods *[bútcra](dictionary.html?q=b%C3%BAtcra)* 'beeswax' and *cravtoñí* 'woven bag') are flagged as candidates for a future page-image re-transcription pass.

Maroto also left unpublished manuscripts (e.g., a 1973 manuscript referenced by Quesada Pacheco 1994) and worked to teach the language to younger generations. Constenla and Maroto jointly designed a practical Boruca orthography in 1979 (the direct ancestor of this project's own apostrophe-for-glottal-stop convention, §2.1 of the reference grammar), and Constenla's later anthology *Poesía tradicional indígena costarricense* (UCR) — drawing on fieldwork conducted from 1972 onward — recovered only five Boruca songs in total (four lullabies and one love song), a genre-specific scarcity he contrasts explicitly with the comparatively strong survival of spoken narrative among the same fluent speakers, suggesting language attrition did not proceed evenly across oral-literary genres. In 1982 the community received a donated broadcasting station, "Radio Cultural Boruca," explicitly intended to air Boruca-language programming as a preservation measure — this attempt failed, and by the 2000s the station broadcast only in Spanish (Castro 2008). Miguel A. Quesada Pacheco, a non-native fluent speaker, became the dominant researcher of this period: he published the pedagogical grammar *Hablemos [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)* (1995, MEP/Departamento de Educación Indígena), the narrative collection *Narraciones borucas* (1996), an article on the Boruca adjective (1997–98), and, with Carmen Rojas Chaves, the *Diccionario Boruca-Español / Español-Boruca* (Editorial UCR; the copy consulted for this project is the 2013 printing, though the underlying project and citations elsewhere date to 1999, suggesting at least one intervening edition). The dictionary's own acknowledgments page (p. 9) is a primary source in its own right and adds detail beyond what the grammar papers reported: the research was formally underway by 1989 and the authors describe it as never fully finished, since "cada vez que los autores viajan a la zona encuentran nuevas palabras." The University of Costa Rica specifically funded the 1990 contract of **Doña Celedina Maroto** as a paid Boruca-language informant, who was responsible for compiling vocabulary and reviewing the material collected up to that point — independent confirmation of her role as a language teacher/authority, consistent with the 2020 Patrimonio Brunka Facebook post (Period 7) mourning her death and describing her as a "maestra de idioma BORUCA." A second, independent source corroborates and sharpens this same moment: the community-authored educational booklet *Cácqui yásará [bá](dictionary.html?q=b%C3%A1) / Mi pueblo [Boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)* (MEP Departamento de Educación Intercultural, in collaboration with the Escuela Doris Z. Stone, ca. 2025) states that "a finales de 1990" a formal Boruca-language teaching project began at the Escuela Doris Z. Stone — a community member giving lessons in Boruca as a second language to schoolchildren — and that this program "fue tomando cuerpo y fue asumido por el Departamento de Educación Indígena en 1995," the same year that department began operating. This independently dates and names the mechanism (a school-based second-language program, not yet a full revitalization curriculum) behind the dictionary-informant activity already documented for 1989–1990, and gives a precise institutional handoff date (1995) not available from the dictionary's acknowledgments alone. The booklet itself states the school was "creada en 1887," which is very likely a typographical error (probably intended as 1987, given the school bears Doris Stone's name and Stone's own fieldwork postdates 1887 by decades) and should be treated as unverified pending a corroborating source. The acknowledgments also name additional informant-contributors not otherwise documented in this project's sources: **Doña Santa Carmela Lázaro** (deceased by the time of publication, noted "q.d.D.g."), **Doña Josefina González**, alongside the already-known Doña Paulina Leiva, Doña Eligia Maroto, Don Ernesto González, and Don Nemesio González. A precise earlier data point, cited by Constenla via Rojas Chaves (1992:37), gives **exactly ten fluent Boruca speakers as of 1992, all over 50 and six of them over 75**, alongside roughly fifty more who understood but could not fluently speak the language — meaning the fluent-speaker count roughly halved in the five years between this 1992 count and Quesada Pacheco's (1997–98) figure below. Quesada Pacheco (1997–98) reported roughly 5 fluent speakers and ~30 semi-speakers in the mid-1990s. J. Diego Quesada's case study "Synopsis of a Boruca Terminal Speaker" (written 1997, published Amerindia 25, 2000) documents this endpoint concretely: the Boruca district held roughly 2,500 people, a dozen passive understanders, and six elderly active speakers, ranging from Doña Paulina Leiva (fluent, linguistically vigorous in her 90s, later helped translate the Gospel of John) to terminal speaker Don Ernesto González, whose regular Boruca use ceased after his wife (a fluent speaker) died in 1984; his speech by the late 1990s showed severe discourse-level breakdown (code-switching into Spanish, referential incoherence) despite intact syntax and phonology. Don Ernesto died in 1998. Espíritu Santo Maroto's own manuscripts were posthumously edited and published by Quesada Pacheco as *Lengua o dialecto [boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474) o brúnkajk* (1999) — and that 1999 critical edition (now processed for this project; see `boruca_maroto_rojas_synthesis.md`) resolves a question this document previously left open. **Espíritu Santo Maroto Rojas (1904–1981)** — police sergeant turned self-taught community philologist, who by his own 1975 autobiography began teaching Boruca in the 1970s "viendo el fatal deterioro del boruca," produced multiple handwritten teaching notebooks with dated pedagogical diaries (1973), and was hired by the Universidad de Costa Rica in 1975 to teach the language for roughly nine months (five in Santo Domingo de Heredia, four in Llorente de Tibás) — married **Doña Paulina Leiva Morales** (b. 8 June 1908) in the early 1920s. She is the same Doña Paulina Leiva who recurs throughout this project as dictionary informant, Song V performer (Constenla), toponymy informant (Quesada Pacheco), and Shécra narrator (Rojas Chaves), making her the single most-attested individual in the project's entire source base — and now confirmed as the wife, not merely a contemporary, of the community's own 20th-century language teacher. Their daughter, **Doña Eligia Maroto Leiva**, is the same Doña Eligia Maroto documented above as one of Castro's three surviving semi-speakers in 2007, married to fellow semi-speaker Don Nemesio González — meaning three generations of language documentation in this project (Espíritu Santo's teaching career, Paulina's decades as the primary informant, and Eligia's status as one of the last semi-speakers) trace back to a single family. Maroto's presentación also documents his own translation methodology from a large unpublished corpus of Boruca-language radio-script translations (*Escuela para todos*), giving concrete worked examples of semantic-extension and derivational neologism strategies (e.g. *[huí](dictionary.html#e-3323)* 'saber' → *huíí* 'científico'; *[huic](dictionary.html?q=huic)* 'espíritu' + *[chíjgua](dictionary.html#e-3023)* → *[huic chíjgua](dictionary.html#e-3327)* 'cámara fotográfica') alongside cases of Spanish syntactic calquing — the most direct primary-source evidence in the project for how a native speaker actually coined new vocabulary, and a natural reference point for the still-undrafted neologism framework. He also proposed his own diacritic mark (˅) for stress/glottal quality in his 1970s notebooks, independently of and prior to the formal Constenla & Maroto practical alphabet of 1979.

## Period 6: Native-Speaker Extinction and the Semi-Speaker Twilight (2001–2010)

The last fully fluent native generation died out in this decade. Per prior project findings (corroborated by Castro 2008, written from a 2007 conference paper): the last fully native speaker died in **2003**. By 2007, Castro documented only **three semi-speakers** remaining — Don Nemesio González (82), Doña Eligia Maroto (75, married to Nemesio, which had helped the couple keep the language in practical use), and Doña Angela González (74, described by the other two as not speaking "correctly"). Doña Eligia died on **21 August 2007**, shortly after Castro's fieldwork — this matches the previously known fact that the spouse of the last remaining semi-speaker died in 2007. Castro (2008) documents demographic context via Solano (2000): the Boruca and Curré reserves held 2,017 indigenous inhabitants (1,386 in Boruca, 631 in Curré; 3,936 total including non-indigenous residents), with 48.8% of reserve residents lacking indigenous ancestry — a fact Borucas themselves cite as a cause of language loss. Sociolinguistically, Boruca had narrowed to a "secret code" function: elderly semi-speakers used it to communicate covertly in front of outsiders (e.g., warning a community member during an attempted robbery), and a handful of specific words (e.g., for bathroom needs) persisted among young children as an in-group code, without broader competence. Elementary schools offered Boruca classes, but instruction stopped at the secondary level, and English was increasingly prioritized because of tourism revenue from Boruca crafts. Applying Bauman's (1980) five-point vitality scale, Castro (2008) classifies Boruca as flatly **extinct** — negative on every vitality indicator, with (semi-)speakers constituting only 0.03% of the total population. Craftsmanship (weaving, masks) rather than language had become the primary marker of Boruca identity by this point.

## Period 7: Post-Extinction and the Digital Revival Movement (2010–present, 2026)

None of the three academic source documents (all pre-2010) directly cover this period, so most of the trend-line reasoning below is extrapolated from the trajectory documented in Periods 5–6 and should be treated as inference. However, social-media evidence gathered directly for this project (reviewed with the project owner, 2020–2025) confirms that a real, organized revival effort now exists and gives this period a documentary basis the earlier academic sources lack. Key attested developments: **Patrimonio Cultural de Boruca**, a Facebook page explicitly framed around cultural/linguistic patrimony, was active by June 2020, publicly mourning the death of Doña Celedina Maroto — described as a "maestra de idioma BORUCA" (teacher of the Boruca language) and praised for her "gran aporte cultural" and fight "por no dejar que se pierda el idioma BRUN"KAJC" ('so that the Brunkajc language is not lost') — direct evidence that dedicated language teachers were still active as late as 2020, a generation after Period 6's extinction assessment. A community Facebook comment thread from around 2020 (on a post by Gilbert González Maroto) shows fluent-register, vocabulary-dense Boruca-Spanish code-switching in ordinary casual conversation (Edixon Mora's comment, referencing *cuchumbo*, *yubú'*, *dibícun xít*, *guacho*, *chocao*, *muasenca*), suggesting the language persists at least as an active lexical/identity register among some community members, even if not as a fully spoken first language. By June 2023, community mourning posts (e.g., for Yesenia Mora Lázaro, described as a "gran mujer de Lucha") continued to use Boruca self-identifiers and group names ("Cagrú'rójc Bruncájc"). Most significantly, an organized language-revival collective, **Instagram account *muntabrunkajctegat*** ("Colectivo de lengua brunca"), was active as of mid-2025 with the explicit slogan "Nuestro idioma está vivo, los seres de ceniza estamos vivos" ('Our language [is](dictionary.html?q=is) alive, the ash-beings are alive') — its content includes archival maps, language-class recordings, and materials for Día Internacional de la Lengua Materna (International Mother Language Day, 21 February) events on technology's role in multilingual learning. Cultural-heritage organizations (e.g., **Alma Tica**, July 2026) continue to publicize related traditional-arts preservation (mask-carving workshops under Leonel Gómez's "[Cró](dictionary.html#e-2722) [Coñát](dictionary.html#e-2601)" initiative), framing craft transmission and language transmission as parts of the same preservation project — continuing the Period 6 pattern (Castro 2008) of craftsmanship functioning alongside language as a marker of [Boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474) identity, but now explicitly networked with renewed language-specific activism rather than substituting for it. **The current formal status of the MEP curriculum — now resolved (2026 addition).** The Ministerio de Educación Pública's own *Programa de Estudios de Lengua Boruca: Primero y Segundo Ciclos* (2017, 123pp; full synthesis in `boruca_mep_2017_curriculum_synthesis.md`) directly answers what the previous paragraph could only flag as an open question. The curriculum was formally, institutionally active as of 2017 and — in its own words — "más de dos décadas de enseñanza de la lengua boruca en las escuelas de los territorios de Boruca y Curré han dado como resultado una recuperación de la lengua, muy lenta pero ya notoria" (more than two decades of Boruca-language teaching in Boruca and Curré schools have produced a recovery of the language — very slow, but already noticeable) — a direct, dated, institutionally-authored positive assessment, not proof of new fluent first-language speakers but real evidence the school-based effort begun in the 1990s (Period 5 above) had a measurable effect by two decades later. The document also gives this project its fullest legal/policy timeline for indigenous-language education in Costa Rica: the 1989 decree recognizing indigenous languages as "lenguas locales"; Costa Rica's 1990 and 1992 ratifications of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and ILO Convention 169; the 1997 agreement formally establishing "Lengua Indígena" and "Cultura Indígena" as distinct mandatory school subjects; the 1999 constitutional reform recognizing Costa Rica's multilingual character; and — most significant for this project's own framing — **Decree N°37801-MEP (2013)**, which formally lists Cabécar, Bribri, Ngöbe, Buglé, and Maleku as Costa Rica's currently-active ("vigentes") indigenous mother tongues while stating explicitly that Teribe and **Boruca are "en proceso de revitalización"** — an official government classification independently corroborating this entire document's "functionally extinct, actively being revived" framing, now with direct legal-source backing. A 2014 Constitutional Court ruling further confirms Lengua Materna/Cultura Indígena are mandatory, not optional, subjects, with the same legal status as mathematics. The 2017 curriculum's own drafting commission was coordinated by **Carmen Rojas Chaves** — the same scholar behind the 1988 Diablitos paper, the 1992 word-formation study, and the 1999 dictionary — with named contributors from the Leiva, Maroto, Rojas, González, and Lázaro families already central to this project's documentation, plus newly-documented figures **don Nicanor Lázaro** and **doña Anita Rojas**, both used as biography/oral-history subjects in the curriculum's upper grades alongside Paulina Leiva herself — a fitting illustration of the same handful of families simultaneously serving, across three generations now, as both the documented sources and the documenting/teaching authorities of this project's entire record.

Confirming whether any elderly rememberers/semi-speakers survive as of 2026 would still require sources this research pass did not have access to — but the digital-community evidence, together with the MEP curriculum's own self-reported results, establishes that Boruca's post-extinction status is not simply passive heritage memory; there is active, self-organized revival demand and a formally active, decades-long institutional teaching program, which is the central premise this project (and its companion orthography-standardization work in the reference grammar, §2.1) is built to support.

This period's strongest documentary evidence, however, is not social media but a full curricular artifact: *Cácqui yásará [bá](dictionary.html?q=b%C3%A1) / Mi pueblo [Boruca](dictionary.html#e-4474)* (subtitled *Flora, fauna, tradiciones y costumbres, educación*), a 93-page bilingual educational booklet produced by community teachers under the MEP Departamento de Educación Intercultural in coordination with the Escuela Doris Z. Stone (created as a PDF file in May 2025, per its embedded metadata). Unlike every other source in this project, it is a first-person, present-tense community document, not a retrospective academic account of the language's decline — its own introduction states plainly that the native language "se encuentra en proceso de revitalización" (is in a process of revitalization) and frames the entire book as material to help "las generaciones actuales y futuras" reclaim territory, history, and language together. It dedicates a full unit to language rights ("Derecho a nuestra lengua autóctona") built around the concept of *[Brugrá](dictionary.html#e-2316)* (the legendary guardian spirit already documented in this project's vocabulary database) as a metaphor for the language itself — "el idioma boruca duerme pero día a día despierta en el corazón de cada brúnca" ('the Boruca language sleeps but wakes a little more each day in the heart of every Brúnca person') — and lists concrete strengthening actions already in practice (radio programming, community celebrations, ADI involvement) alongside asks directed at MEP and international bodies. It also embeds real linguistic material used pedagogically: a complete cardinal-number system 1–10 plus a productive base-10 counting method for compound numbers (with connective particles *[yraón](dictionary.html#e-4442)* 'plus/and' and *[yáv dóvsh](dictionary.html#e-4443)* 'equals'), 32 previously undocumented vocabulary items now added to `boruca_vocabulary.db` (foods, edible plants and fungi, and 10 named legendary/guardian figures), and a complete original Boruca-language song ("Un paseo por la montaña," by community member Oscar Leiva M.) — a rare connected-text specimen in a documentary record that is otherwise almost entirely wordlists. Taken together, this booklet is best read as evidence that the "digital revival movement" documented via social media has a parallel, formal, in-school counterpart as of the mid-2020s — not proof of new fluent speakers, but proof of an active, current, community-authored pedagogical program treating language reclamation as inseparable from land rights, ethnobotany, and cultural memory.

**Diachronic stability across the Thiel/Stone record (2026 addition).** A comparative analysis of the Thiel 1882 wordlist, Stone's own Appendix C, and the modern ~4,707-entry dictionary (see `boruca_thiel_stone_comparative_synthesis.md`) finds that Boruca's core subsistence vocabulary — corn, tobacco, deer, fish, hammock, pejibaye, wax, monkey — shows striking phonetic stability across the 31 items independently attested by 4 or more of Stone's 8 historical sources, in several cases essentially unchanged from Valentini's 1862 transcriptions through to the modern dictionary (a 144-to-164-year span). Corn's "k/c-up" root and deer's "suturíc" root are the clearest examples, each stable across seven and four independent 19th/20th-century sources respectively, with modern forms nearly identical to the earliest recorded ones. A conservative, heuristic-based comparison of Thiel's 1882 Boruca forms against the modern dictionary (296 exact-gloss matches) found roughly a quarter showing high orthographic retention using a strict scoring method — flagged in the synthesis document as a lower-bound estimate, since the heuristic misclassifies some clear cognates (e.g. "uj zí" 1882 → "ujsí" modern, 'barba') as replacements. This pattern of long-term lexical stability in core vocabulary, set against the well-documented 20th-century collapse in intergenerational transmission and fluent-speaker counts elsewhere in this timeline, suggests the language's decline has been primarily a break in transmission rather than internal lexical instability — the vocabulary itself, where it has survived, has changed comparatively little.
