A Structured Reference Grammar of Boruca (Brunka)

Compiled from eight academic sources for language-revival and pedagogical use

1. Overview of Boruca / Brunka

Boruca (also called Brunka or Brúnkajk) is a Chibchan language of the southern Pacific region of Costa Rica, historically spoken in the area of Buenos Aires, Puntarenas province, near the Panama border, in two reserves: Boruca and Curré (Castro 2010, "The Boruca Verbal Dimension"; Castro 2010, "The Complex Clause in Boruca"). Genetically, Boruca belongs to the Isthmian branch of the Southern Chibchan languages, alongside Teribe and Cuna, and — like those two — has no immediate sister language, which some scholars take as a sign that it may constitute a branch of its own within Chibchan (Castro 2012, citing Quesada 2007). Other Isthmian-branch relatives spoken in the region include Bribri and Cabécar (Viceita branch) and Guaymí/Ngäbere and Bocotá (Guaymian branch); the now-extinct Chánguena and Dorasque belonged to the Doracic branch (Quesada 2007, cited in Castro 2012).

Boruca is functionally extinct as a spoken first language. Sources across the corpus disagree slightly on the precise timeline, which itself is informative for revival planning: Quesada Pacheco (1997–98) describes roughly five fluent speakers and about thirty semi-speakers still able to produce isolated phrases in the mid-1990s; by Castro's 2010 account, the last fully native speaker died in 2003, leaving a single remaining semi-speaker (whose semi-speaker spouse died in 2007). Quesada Pacheco (2011, "La causatividad en el boruca") likewise describes the language, as of that writing, as known only passively by a few adults. All current descriptive work therefore rests on a combination of (a) legacy field materials — most importantly Pittier's 1892–96 manuscripts (published 1941) and Constenla & Maroto's Leyendas y tradiciones borucas (1979/1986) — and (b) elicitation with the last semi-speakers, notably Nemesio González (used by Castro) and doña Paulina Leiva, Espíritu Santo Maroto, Isolina Céspedes, and Ernesto González (the four narrators whose traditional stories underlie Abarca González's frequency study). Revitalization efforts by the Costa Rican Ministry of Public Education, going back to the 1970s and including Quesada Pacheco's own teaching grammar Hablemos boruca (1995) and school materials (1998), are acknowledged by their own author as having had limited success in producing active speakers, which is part of the motivation for the more recent linguistic documentation this grammar draws on.

2. Phonology & Orthography Notes

Project orthographic standard. For all materials produced under this revival project, the glottal stop /ʔ/ is written with the apostrophe ('), not <v> or any other symbol. See §2.1 for the rationale and supporting evidence.

Phonological documentation across these sources is thin — none of the eight papers is a dedicated phonological description, and this is a genuine gap for revival material (see §8). What can be gleaned:

  • Practical alphabet. Quesada Pacheco (2011) describes the "official practical alphabet" used in modern Boruca texts as largely following Spanish orthographic conventions, with two notable exceptions: <sh> represents the postalveolar fricative /ʃ/, and <v> represents the glottal stop /ʔ/. The velar nasal consonant /ŋ/ is written <n̈> (a tilde-n), though other publications have used the Greek letter eta (η) for the same sound when the tilde-n was not typographically available.
  • A second alphabet chart appears in Abarca González's frequency study (based on Constenla & Maroto's practical alphabet), giving a fuller consonant inventory organized by place and manner: bilabial, dental/alveolar, palatal, velar, and laryngeal stops, affricates, fricatives, a simple vibrant/flap, and nasals; the vowel inventory is the five-vowel system /i, e, a, o, u/ (high i/u, mid e/o, low a).
  • Tone. Boruca has a two-way tonal contrast, high and low, represented in the practical alphabet by an acute accent (´) for high tone and its absence for low tone (Abarca González; also referenced by Quesada Pacheco 2011 in connection with tonal harmony, see §5.3). Quesada Pacheco (2011) notes that Boruca shows tonal harmony, whereby a verbal suffix takes on a high tone when the monosyllabic verb root has low tone, and vice versa (Quesada Pacheco 1985, cited therein).
  • Suprasegmental causative alternation. Directly relevant to phonology-morphology interaction: certain monosyllabic low-tone verbs become causative/transitive when the root shifts to high tone (see §5.3 below) — apparently a typologically unique mechanism not otherwise attested in the Chibchan family per Quesada Pacheco's comparison.
  • Morphophonemic deletion. Castro (2012) notes that the infinitive marker -í / -íh is deleted when TAM markers are added to the verb stem, and that the root-final /k/ of ték ('to talk') is lost before the past-tense marker -krá (i.e., tegrá rather than tekrá) because speakers reanalyze that /k/ as belonging to the tense morpheme rather than the root (Quesada & Quesada Pacheco 1995).

Beyond these scattered notes, there is no systematic phonemic inventory, no discussion of syllable structure, minimal pairs, or stress, and no phonetic description of vowel or consonant realization in the corpus. Any revival-oriented phonology will need to draw directly on Quesada Pacheco's teaching materials (Hablemos boruca, 1995) or Constenla & Maroto (1986), neither of which was among the source texts read for this synthesis.

2.1 Orthography standardization: the glottal stop

The problem. No single symbol for the glottal stop is used consistently across the sources, or in current community usage. This is not a minor typographic detail — the glottal stop is phonemically load-bearing in Boruca (it distinguishes words and, per §2's tonal-harmony and morphophonemic notes, interacts with tone and suffix boundaries), so an unstable notation makes both the academic corpus and any new material harder to read and to search.

Evidence of the inconsistency:

  • Within the formal academic literature itself. Quesada Pacheco (2011, "La causatividad en el boruca") gives <v> as the practical-alphabet symbol for /ʔ/. But his own earlier work does not follow this: "El adjetivo boruca" (1997–98) glosses examples with the apostrophe throughout — anté'shi 'leaned', bi'shi 'buried', cáchi'shi 'covered', tutsi' 'stretched' — and the separately sourced 2,255-entry vocabulary spreadsheet (Bruncajc Vocabulary.xlsx) likewise uses the apostrophe as its working notation (a' 'to lower', a'chí' 'decaído, débil', a'chí'ua 'bordón'). In other words, the apostrophe is already the de facto standard in the actual linguistic data, independent of what the cited "official" alphabet chart says.
  • In current community usage (see the social-media examples reviewed with the project owner, June 2020–October 2025). Boruca-affiliated pages and users write the same sound, or a closely related one, with a variety of ad hoc, keyboard-typable symbols rather than <v>: a caret (^) in forms like Cagrú^rójc (Kabrú Rójc Brunkajc Boruca, June 2023), Oc^sa for 'Osa' (Saúl Morales, October 2025), yubú^ and rít co^ (comment thread on Gilbert González Maroto's post, March 2020); and a straight double-quote in Brun"kajc (Patrimonio Brunka, June 2020). None of these community authors appear to be using <v>, and no two of them use exactly the same symbol as each other — the community itself has not converged on a shared convention, which is precisely the kind of gap a revival project can usefully close.

Decision. For this project, the glottal stop is written with the apostrophe ('). Rationale:

  1. Continuity with the existing documentary record. It matches the working notation already used in the vocabulary data and in Quesada Pacheco's own descriptive (non-chart) practice, so adopting it doesn't create a new split between "project spelling" and "source spelling" — it resolves an existing one in favor of the more heavily attested option.
  2. Keyboard and platform portability. The apostrophe is a standard ASCII character available on every keyboard and rendering correctly across phones, social platforms, and plain text, unlike the caret-based or accent-stacking workarounds visible in the community posts above, which likely arose precisely because contributors didn't have easy access to a dedicated glottal-stop character.
  3. Intuitive iconicity for a Spanish-literate audience. A stop/break mark reads naturally as a stop, which is a small but real advantage for learners approaching Boruca through Spanish literacy.
  4. It reduces, rather than adds to, the fragmentation already visible both across the academic sources (<v> vs. apostrophe) and across community usage (caret vs. quotation mark vs. other diacritics) — see also §8, point 9, on cross-source terminological inconsistency generally.

Open item. It is not yet confirmed whether the caret (^) seen in the Facebook examples is being used by those writers for the same sound as the apostrophe in the academic sources, or for something else (e.g., nasalization or a tone-related mark) — some practical Chibchan-family orthographies do use caret-like marks for nasal vowels rather than glottal stop. Before publishing learner-facing materials that silently "correct" community spellings to the apostrophe, it would be worth confirming this with a fluent reader or with the maintainers of the muntabrunkajctegat / Patrimonio Brunka pages, both to avoid conflating two distinct sounds and as a matter of respectful engagement with the community already doing this work.

Historical footnote. The apostrophe's prevalence in the corpus is not simply an academic inconsistency — it has a documented history. Quesada Pacheco (2011) notes that Maroto Rojas's original practical alphabet (the one underlying the earliest manuscript materials) was used "de manera inconsistente" ('inconsistently') before Constenla & Maroto's 1979 regularized version fixed <v> as the official glottal-stop symbol. That means the apostrophe forms found in Maroto's own manuscripts, in the 1997–98 adjective paper (which draws on those manuscripts), and in the vocabulary spreadsheet are best understood as a pre-standardization convention that simply never fully died out in practice — not a stray typo. This project's adoption of the apostrophe can be read as continuing that older working practice rather than inventing a new one.

3. Nominal Morphology: Adjectives

The fullest treatment of adjective morphology is Quesada Pacheco's "El adjetivo boruca" (1997–98), which builds on and revises earlier work by Pittier (1896/1941), Arroyo (1966), Constenla (in Constenla & Maroto 1979), and especially Rojas (1992). Quesada Pacheco identifies seven productive adjectival suffixes, each with characteristic semantic domains, plus notes on a fading grade/intensity system.

3.1 The suffix -uá (nominalizing / instrumental, "perfective" reading)

Forms nouns denoting the instrument used to perform an action; found abundantly in coinages for modern objects:

  • huic 'soul' + chij 'to capture' → huic chijguá 'camera'
  • seján 'cold' + sodíj 'to make' → seján sádijuá 'refrigerator'
  • di' 'water' + broj 'to boil' → di' brojguá 'coffee-maker'

A secondary, reduced function is the gerund: cun̈uá 'flying'.

3.2 The suffix -shi (positional/stative adjectives)

Marks adjectives of position, state, or situation: anté'shi 'leaning, reclined', becshi 'mounted, straddling', bi'shi 'buried', cáchi'shi 'covered', crástacshi 'legs spread', shuajshi 'face-down'. Some bases accept both -shi and -uá, with a systematic contrast: -shi yields an adjective denoting position, -uá yields a noun denoting the instrument of the action, e.g. anté' 'to lean' → anté'shi 'leaned' (adj.) vs. anté'uá 'quilt' (n.); cap 'to sleep' → capshi 'asleep' vs. cabuá 'bedroom'; úshe 'to dress' → ushéshi 'dressed' vs. úsheuá 'garment'. The suffix has phonetically conditioned allomorphs: -sí after dentals (tutsi' 'stretched') and -í after the velar nasal (cuiní 'placed', from cuín 'to put'; yacaní 'led by the hand').

3.3 The suffix -rani (attenuative degree, contested)

Rojas (1992) analyzed -rani as marking an attenuated ("somewhat X") degree; Quesada Pacheco's data from Maroto's manuscripts confirm this general pattern (but 'to sweeten' → budrani 'half-sweet'; tura' 'to rot' → tura'rani 'somewhat rotten'; rut 'to burn' → rudrani 'somewhat burnt') but also found some informants confusing the attenuative with the intensive grade (§3.4) — evidence, in the author's view, that the language's grade system was already breaking down among the last speakers. -rani is noted as considerably more productive than the intensive suffix that follows.

3.4 The suffix -ón/ (intensive degree)

Marks an intensified ("very X") reading, but — unlike -rani — restricted to specific semantic classes: states of matter (bal'gón/ 'very bitter', turavón/ 'very rotten'), textures (ajgán/ 'very strong/tough', crasán/ 'very resistant'), temperatures (ro'gón/ 'very hot/burning', sejón/ 'very cold'), and mental/emotional states (shugón/ 'very nervous', yrojón/ 'very tired'). Adjectives outside these classes cannot take -ón/; intensity for them is instead expressed periphrastically with the adverb cuín/ 'very'. The manuscripts of Espíritu Santo Maroto even show -ón/-marked adjectives reinforced redundantly with cuín/ (e.g. cuín/crasón/ 'very resistant'), which Quesada Pacheco interprets as a sign of ongoing desemanticization/loss of this paradigm.

3.5 The suffix -át (the most productive; "inherent quality")

By far the most common and semantically broadest adjectival suffix, marking inherent qualities across several domains: colors (drubát 'red', hua'gát 'green', yiát 'yellow'), shapes (basát 'round', cajbát 'horizontal'), states of matter (canát 'hard', cáxenát 'raw', chevát 'sick'), weather/atmospheric phenomena (ba'gát 'clear/dawn-related', seját 'cold', tebejdát 'moon-related'), positions (beát 'hidden', ijxiát 'crouched'), ages (bojgát 'young [male]', bushát 'young [female]', ramát 'female', xidát 'newborn'), and dimensions (biogát 'long', manát 'wide', euját 'short'). Pittier (1896/1941) already noted this suffix's productivity, though without discussing the rest of the system.

3.6 The suffix -é (compositional adjectives)

Applies to nominal or verbal roots to form adjectives denoting the base's implicit quality (ram 'female' → ramé 'female [adj.]'; drup 'to blaze' → drubé 'brilliant'). Distinctively, many -é adjectives are compounds of two roots whose combined meaning conveys iterativity/habituality: xu' 'to flee' + dibin/ → xu'é dibin/ 'skittish, wild'; tec 'to speak' + yén/ 'to show' → tegué yén/ 'chatty, talkative'.

3.7 The suffix -i'shi (negative present participle)

A low-productivity suffix forming negated participial adjectives: bag 'to have' → bagui'shi 'lacking, without'; tec 'to speak' → teguí'shi 'non-speaking, mute'.

3.8 Semantic hierarchy proposed by Quesada Pacheco

The paper concludes with a hierarchical semantic schema organizing the adjectival system around binary features:

  • [+ inherent quality] → -át
  • [– inherent quality] splits into:
    • [+ resultative] → [+ perfective] -uá vs. [– perfective] -shi
    • [– resultative] → [+ intensity] -ón/ vs. [– intensity], further split by [+ process] -rani vs. [– process] -cá (a "state" suffix noted in passing but not treated in depth in this paper)

This schema explains apparent near-synonyms that Spanish translation collapses but Boruca formally distinguishes, e.g. ejtcá '(is currently) thin' vs. ejdát '(is inherently) thin' vs. ejduá 'thinned/made thin'; cutcá '(is) full' vs. cudát 'pot-bellied'. The author stresses this schema is provisional and that further study is needed, particularly around the productivity of -uá with transitive verb bases (possibly linked to transitivity marking generally, an area the paper flags as unstudied) and the fact that -cá-final adjectives freely co-occur with -rani, -ón/, and -át but never with -shi.

4. Determiners/Clitics: the ki Clitic

The clitic ki is one of the most distinctive and grammatically productive elements in Boruca, and is treated in depth by Quesada & Quesada Pacheco (1995, "Boruca ki: de definido a específico y la hipótesis de las funciones contiguas"). Ki's behavior is unusual among Chibchan languages generally, since the word class "article" is not common in the family — Boruca appears to be innovating one from scratch, which the authors take as one of several signs (along with the infinitive marker, see §5.1) that Boruca may be typologically distinctive within Chibchan.

4.1 Proposed etymology

The authors propose that ki arose through resegmentation of sequences ending in root-final -k plus the third-person/demonstrative marker i (< proto-Chibchan hi 'thing, that'). This i independently gives rise, depending on whether it attaches to a verbal or nominal base, to (a) the free third-person pronoun í and a "lemmatic" citation-form proclitic, when attached to verbs, and (b) a nominalizer and — via reanalysis — the article ki, when attached to nouns. Proposed reanalyses include: kapsúxk-i > kapsux-ki ('this sleepy-one' → 'the sleepy one'); so'tóxk-i > sotróx-ki ('this old-one' → 'the old lady'); brúnkaxk-i > brúnkax-ki ('this Boruca-one' → 'the Boruca [person]'). The same -k loss process is independently evidenced by the verb ték 'to speak' losing its final /k/ before the past-tense marker -krá (→ tegrá*), which the authors take as reinforcing evidence for the resegmentation hypothesis.

4.2 The Function Contiguity Hypothesis and the grammaticalization path

Drawing on Blansitt's (1988) "function contiguity hypothesis" (originally developed for allative/dative markers), the authors argue that ki's evolving functions trace a single continuum of increasing topicality/specificity, moving through the following stages, each attested with textual examples:

  1. Definite article on simple nouns in subject, object, and oblique position: tebek ki 'the snake', tebek kí kawí'ta brúnkax tá 'The snake lives in Boruca.'
  2. Extension to demonstrative-headed NPs: ki cliticizes to the more grammaticalized demonstratives wá and yá (e.g. wá ki 'this one'), but not to the less grammaticalized wé' and tí' (later developments that arose after ki itself).
  3. Extension to noun+adjective phrases and relative clauses, where ki attaches to the whole phrase, not just the head noun: kútni moréng ki 'the good pig'; ú sodíxkrin̈ ki morénra 'the house that they built is good.'
  4. Extension to proper names: Kwasráng ki é'tse kóntox(ki) ái'kra 'Kuasrán killed a man' — a step attested cross-linguistically (cf. Sp. la María, It. il Carlo).
  5. Grammaticalization as subject marker on personal pronouns, now nearly obligatory: át ki (1SG), bá ki (2SG), í ki (3SG), and their plural counterparts dit róxk ki, bi' tóxk ki, i róxk ki. Notably ki does not appear when these same referents are objects or obliques, nor when the emphatic/focus marker ang is present (át ki báxtkrin 'he hit me' is ungrammatical with ki on the pronominal object).
  6. Optional/discourse-conditioned marking of oblique arguments, correlating with benefactive/malefactive role and discourse prominence — the same referent (e.g. the culture-hero Cuasrán) can appear with or without ki depending on its relative topicality in context, a pattern the authors compare to differential object marking with Spanish a.
  7. Combination with the indefinite article é'tse. Ki co-occurs with the indefinite marker é'tse when the indefinite referent is nonetheless discursively specified/emphasized (e.g. with evaluative adjectives like brát 'big', kri'é 'beautiful', sungróx 'old', búj, só'). It is excluded when é'tse denotes an age ('one year'), when relf 'only' or dó'l 'like' are present, in adjective sequences, in possessor+possessed constructions, before objects of postpositions, in indefinite copular complements, or when the emphasizers abí/ang are present. This distributional split shows ki's function here is specifically to mark specificity, not (in)definiteness per se — capturing the ambiguity of a phrase like Sp. "me caso con una francesa" (a French woman I know vs. one I don't).
  8. Specificity marking in temporal expressions: duxbi'k ki tá 'last night', tsáx ki tá 'today', é'tse dék ki tá 'once (upon a time)'.
  9. Further grammaticalization as a subordinator, introducing complement clauses with verbs of saying/thinking (verba cogitandi et dicendi): át ki tekrá ki... 'I said that...'; ógé bin ték dóxkta ki... 'You have all heard that...'. This use is plausibly a Spanish-influenced calque of que but is argued to be independently supported by ki's prior grammaticalization as a specificity marker, paralleling how demonstratives become subordinators in Germanic languages (cf. Eng. that, Ger. dass < demonstrative das).

The authors summarize the overall path as: REANALYSIS > DEFINITENESS > SPECIFICATION, with the last stage branching into three further specializations — (a) marking indefinite-but-specific referents, (b) marking pronominal subjects, and (c) functioning as a subordinator — each requiring that ki have already reached the "specifier" stage, which the authors present as evidence for the unidirectionality principle of grammaticalization theory.

5. The Verbal System

The most extensive treatment of Boruca verbal morphology is Castro's "The Boruca Verbal Dimension" (Amerindia 2012), supplemented by Quesada Pacheco's "La causatividad en el boruca" (2011) for causative constructions and Abarca González's frequency study for usage patterns in narrative.

5.1 Verb structure and infinitives

Boruca verbs are not divisible into morphological classes (unlike Spanish's -ar/-er/-ir classes, or Teribe's static/dynamic classes); Boruca verbs are recognizable only on semantic (state/process/action) and syntactic (VP head, sentence-final position) grounds. Sample prototypical verbs by class: state — tsá' 'be cold', jióng 'be hot', jri'ká 'be alive', kohtká 'be dead'; process — koht 'die', tura' 'rot', uh 'melt', kwik 'dance', tabájeng 'sing', tek 'speak', kap 'sleep'; action — ái' 'kill', báht 'hit', shité' 'break'.

Distinctively among Chibchan languages, Boruca has a dedicated infinitive marker, -í (variant -íh) — a feature otherwise absent from the family and, together with ki (§4), one of the features Castro cites as suggesting Boruca may occupy a separate branch of Chibchan. The infinitive appears immediately after a governing matrix verb or within a subordinated postpositional phrase, e.g. róhk shon-ra já' shon-kra róhk teg-íh 'they gather to talk' (talk-INF). It is deleted when TAM suffixes attach directly to the root, which is offered as morphological evidence of its inflectional (not derivational) status. Curiously, its distribution is intermittent: in near-identical syntactic contexts it sometimes appears and sometimes does not, with no consistency found in the data; Castro speculates this may be phonologically conditioned but leaves this for future study. The infinitive is not the citation form — the bare (uninflected) root is used for that — which raises the possibility that the infinitive marker is itself a kind of inflection rather than a true non-finite citation form.

Full verb-structure template (Castro 2012): [ROOT (INF) (AKT) ~ (MOD) (NEG) (ASP) (SUBJ-IS) (MOOD)]. Concretely this yields possibilities such as: bare root (tec 'to speak/talk'); root+infinitive (teg-íh 'to talk'); root+Aktionsart+negation+aspect (ishd-áh-r-í-ng 'he just saw'); root+modality+negation+aspect (juak-ch-í'sh-irá 'did not want to say'); root+mood (ba dabag-á 'you come!').

Boruca verbs classify by valence: intransitive (single argument; SV or VS order, e.g. mang i-ng de-krá róhk i déng ka 'then they went behind him'), transitive (two arguments; SOV/OVS, e.g. e'tsi ramróhk ki é'tsi kóngróhk ki bejáng-kra 'a woman loved a man'), and ditransitive, which require an obligatory postposition to express the third argument (a-ng ba jét báhkang só't ki wí'-kra 'I brought you the four bluegill'). Boruca has no true zero-valence/impersonal verbs: weather predicates use kák ('day/sun/climate') as a grammatical subject, and unspecified subjects are expressed with the generic abí róhk ('people') or the reflexive já' — Castro notes Boruca patterns with Germanic languages here in disallowing null subjects. There is no free-standing copula; instead the TAM suffixes (-ra, -íra, -kra, -kirá) attach directly to nominal, adjectival, locative, or adverbial predicates to yield copula-like nominal/adjectival/locative predications (e.g. bá ki á'd be-rá 'you are my mother'; ógé kahkshí-ra já ki kangkwá's ki ang 'that stone is all open').

5.2 Tense, Aspect, and Mood — the case against a strict tense analysis

This is the centerpiece of Castro's verbal-dimension paper, and it is directly reinforced (using largely the same argument and data) by Castro, Murillo & Quesada's comparative paper "Evolución morfosintáctica en las lenguas chibchas" (2009).

The traditional "Tense Hypothesis." Constenla & Maroto (1986) and Quesada Pacheco (1995, 1996) analyzed the core TAM markers -ra, -kra, -irá, -kirá as a tense system: -ra = non-past (present/future); -kra = perfective past; -irá = imperfective past; -kirá = pluperfect (a "relative" past). They additionally posited a non-obligatory "second series" of pre-tense markers: -ah 'already', -krog 'finish', -cha 'want', -mang 'used to', -ishkong 'have to'.

Castro's critique and reanalysis. Castro argues this hypothesis (i) is asymmetric — it posits a pluperfect with no corresponding perfect; (ii) is not purely temporal, since the -kra/-irá opposition is itself aspectual (perfective/imperfective), a familiar cross-linguistic pattern; and (iii) fails empirically — in narrative texts, -ra-marked verbs regularly occur where a present-tense reading is impossible and only an "actual"/ongoing-narration reading works, and vice versa. She reanalyzes the same four markers as a purely aspectual system:

Imperfective Perfective
Actual (-ra)Durative (-irá)Punctual (-kra)Anterior (-kirá)

In this view -ra is a narrative/actual marker (not "present tense"), and the system's internal-constituency-of-situation logic — rather than temporal sequencing — governs distribution. Perfective (-kra) verbs foreground main narrative events; imperfective (-irá) verbs mark descriptive/background material, following Binnick's (1991) foreground/background distinction.

The special status of -kirá. Castro further shows that -kirá, glossed by earlier authors as "pluperfect," better patterns as a pure anteriority marker not requiring a syntactic point of reference — supported by three independent facts: (1) its frequency is very low relative to the other three markers even in the earliest (1892–96) Pittier texts; (2) the semi-speaker Eligia Maroto reportedly did not recognize or use the form at all (Quesada Pacheco, p.c. 2008); (3) even in the 19th-century Pittier corpus, -kirá tokens are left unglossed (unlike the rest of the text), suggesting difficulty assigning it meaning even then. Castro's conclusion — corroborated in the 2009 comparative paper — is that Boruca was in transition from a tense language to a tenseless (purely aspectual) language, a shift already underway by the late 19th century and evidently incomplete at the point of the language's extinction.

Aktionsart. Castro reanalyzes Constenla & Maroto's "second series" not as a distinct verb series but as Aktionsart (manner-of-action) derivational affixes, distinct in kind from the aspectual (TAM) markers and preceding them in the verb template. These are organized into phases plus two other categories:

  • Imminential (ja, free morpheme) 'about to X': ja-rá ba-ng tru'-í 'you are about to fall'
  • Ingressive (já sa') 'to start'
  • Continuative (do'á) 'to continue': do'á dabag-ra... 'continue to arrive...'
  • Terminative (-krog) 'to finish/stop': i'íng-krog-írá 'they finished tying it'
  • Egressive (-áh) 'to have just X, already': i'ishd-áh-r-í-ng 'they just saw it'
  • Consuetudinary (-mang) 'used to': jaí-mang-kra 'were used to going'
  • Intensity (bra'é, free morpheme) 'excessively': í ki tegu-i bra'é-ra 'he talks excessively'

Notably, Castro traces a plausible diachronic path from causativity to egressive Aktionsart: the fossilized causative collocation chikáh ('to make [a] sound', < chik 'sound' + ah 'make') suggests -ah originally had causative force, which bled into the now much more frequent egressive use of -áh.

Mood and Modality. Imperative is marked by -a (ba tú'a 'eat!') or, alternatively, by the ordinary "actual" aspect marker -ra; imperatives cannot take the regular negator -i'shi-, but instead use the preverbal free particle dí (dí bi-ng dabag-á 'don't come!'). The exhortative cha occurs sentence-initially (chá-ng a'r úhtsa ki shúng-ra... 'come, I want to send...'). Desiderative modality is a bound root suffix (i kabát ék-ír-í-ng...i juak-ch-í'sh-irá 'she didn't want to tell him'); deontic modality ('have to') is expressed by -ishkóng (róhk ái'-ishkóng 'in order to kill it').

5.3 Valence-adjusting operations: causatives in depth

Quesada Pacheco's "La causatividad en el boruca" (2011) is a full monograph-length treatment situating Boruca causatives within Comrie's/Shibatani's/Seiler's typological frameworks (lexical–morphological–analytic continuum; direct vs. indirect causation; control hierarchies). Empirically, Boruca shows three types of causative construction plus a fourth, apparently unique mechanism:

(a) Lexical causatives. No morphological marking distinguishes causative from non-causative use; only context/argument structure disambiguates: Juana cabrá 'Juana sleeps' vs. Juana quí iv huáv quí cabrá 'Juana puts her child to sleep' (lit. 'Juana [makes] her child sleep'); úvshi quí dabavcrá 'the jar broke' vs. Pedro quí úvshi quí dabavcrá 'Pedro broke the jar.'

(b) Morphological causatives (non-productive). Only three verbs were found bearing a clear causative suffix -óv: yo 'to rain' → yóv 'to bathe'; dec 'to go' → degóv 'to scare off'; ajc 'to be strong' → ajgóv 'to hold on'.

(c) Analytic/periphrastic causatives (the most productive type today). An auxiliary verb carries the causative meaning. Two auxiliaries express true causation: sodíj 'to make/do' and ón̈ 'to make, cause' (apparently in free variation), plus shún̈ 'to send, cause' — which the author argues differs subtly from ón̈ in that the causer with shún̈ is not directly/solely responsible for the caused effect, whereas ón̈ implies full causer responsibility. Two further forms express permission: ajt 'to let' (the more common; causing event expressed in the infinitive, e.g. María quí iv huáv róhk quí ajdrá cuijí 'María lets her children play') and evshí 'to let' (an invariant morpheme restricted to imperative/exhortative contexts, e.g. evshín dabagrá 'let him come').

(d) Suprasegmental causatives — a typologically distinctive finding. Quesada Pacheco reports that certain monosyllabic low-tone intransitive verb roots become transitive/causative when the root shifts to high tone, with no other morphological change: ucarás qui cajcrá 'the door opened' vs. María quí úcarás qui cájccra 'María opened the door'; auj quí rajdrá 'the dog goes out' vs. huáv qui auj quí rájdra 'the child lets the dog out'; các qui bejtcrá 'the sun went down' vs. chúp qui béjtcr-n̈ róhk 'they lowered the nest.' The author explicitly states this mechanism appears to be unique to Boruca among the Costa Rican Chibchan languages, even though several of them (e.g. Bribri) also have tonal systems, and treats it as a lexicalized (non-fully-productive) pattern restricted to certain monosyllabic verbs, since some informants rejected extending it to new verbs (\Juana qui huáv qui cábra* 'Juana puts the child to sleep' was rejected).

Anticausatives. A distinct suffix, -rév (allomorph -dév after consonants other than vowels/h), derives anticausative/inchoative verbs from nominal or adjectival bases, restricted semantically to natural-phenomena/biological-process verbs: sej 'cold' → sejrév 'to become cold' (vs. causative séj 'to cool [something]'); yi 'fire' → yirév 'to heat up' (vs. yí 'to heat'); sujc 'old' → sujdév 'to age.' Unlike many cross-linguistic anticausative morphemes, -rév is described as unambiguous (not conflated with reflexive or passive uses).

Productivity assessment. The author's overall conclusion, based on elicitation from remaining speakers, is that analytic causatives are now the preferred/most productive strategy, while the suprasegmental (tonal) causative is unproductive/relic, situating Boruca on the following continuum: Analytic (least direct) → Suprasegmental → Morphological → Lexical (most direct causation). Notably, the paper also reports that Boruca shows no evidence of the prefixal causative morphology reconstructed for proto-Chibchan by Holt (1989) based on comparative evidence from Paya, Cuna, and Kágaba — whether Boruca's -óv suffix is historically related to those prefixes is left as an open question.

5.4 Predicate suffixes: frequency and usage in traditional narratives

Abarca González's thesis-based study directly quantifies which "núcleo predicativo" (predicate-core) suffixes actually occur in traditional Boruca narration, based on 1,516 tokens drawn from stories told by four elder narrators (Espíritu Santo Maroto, Isolina Céspedes, Ernesto González, Paulina Leiva) as recorded in Constenla & Maroto (1979/1986). This is the most directly actionable data in the corpus for sequencing learner materials, since it shows what a fluent narrator actually needs most.

  • The three affirmative finite forms — imperfect (-írá), indefinite/perfective (-crá), and non-past (-ra) — together make up 83.05% of all finite predicate forms and should clearly be prioritized in any teaching sequence, well ahead of the negative counterparts (7.21% combined) or the imperative (0.92%).

  • Periphrastic (auxiliary) verb forms are also rare (63 tokens, 4.16%), dominated by the progressive periphrasis with auxiliary dová ('to do/make' + gerund-like form, 50.79% of periphrases) and the inchoative yá'sá' ('to begin', 30.16%).
  • Non-verbal predicate bases (nominal, adjectival, postpositional-phrase, and adverbial predicates taking the same TAM suffixes — see §5.1 on the copula-like function) account for 10.95% of all predicative forms, confirming that this "adjectival/nominal predication" pattern is a normal, not marginal, part of everyday narrative Boruca.
  • The non-past (-ra) form is semantically the most flexible, attested with habitual, historical-present, actual/ongoing, and future readings — e.g., 45.53% of its uses are "actual" (ongoing at speech time) and 27.09% are historical-present in narrative, a pattern strongly reminiscent of vivid oral-narrative present-tense use in Spanish.
  • Narrator fluency correlates with predicate density: the narrator judged (by Constenla, p.c.) most fluent and expressive, Isolina Céspedes, produced by far the highest average number of predicate forms per narration (113, versus 66.68, 42.25, and 9.33 for the other three), and also showed markedly more use of adjectival predicate bases and historical-present non-past forms — suggesting that predicate density and non-past/historical-present usage may be reasonable proxies for narrative fluency in teaching evaluation.

6. Syntax

6.1 Subject and ergativity

The one source addressing this topic directly, Jack L. Wilson K.'s "Sobre la definición lingüística: el sujeto y el ergativo," turns out on inspection to contain essentially no Boruca-specific data. It is a general theoretical essay, situated within 1980s Government-and-Binding-era generative grammar, critiquing the imprecise and inconsistent use of terms like "subject," "ergative," "nominative," and "absolutive" across the typological literature (citing Chomsky, Fillmore, Dixon, Trask, Comrie, Keenan). Its illustrative language data is drawn almost entirely from Bribri (a Chibchan relative of Boruca, but a distinct language) contrasted with Russian, English, French, and Spanish, to argue that the ergative/accusative distinction is largely terminological rather than a deep typological split, and that Bribri's "ergative" marker -tö likely descends historically from an erstwhile active/passive voice opposition (following Trask's account of the passive-to-ergative diachronic pathway) rather than representing canonical ergativity. No Boruca examples, morphology, or argument-marking data appear anywhere in this paper. For a revival grammar, this source is useful only as background typological framing (i.e., as a caution about how "subject" and "ergative" are used loosely in the broader literature) and should not be cited as evidence about Boruca's own argument-marking system; the actual Boruca subject-marking system is best drawn instead from the ki clitic study (§4) and from Castro's papers, which show that Boruca subjects are marked via the clitics ki, ang, and abí (grammaticalized markers of subject/topic/focus/emphasis, per Castro 2012 fn. 6) rather than by a case system in the Indo-European or ergative-absolutive sense. Boruca does not appear to be ergative in the classical sense in any of the other seven sources; rather, its argument-marking relies on word order (basic SOV, though not rigid) plus the discourse-conditioned clitic system described in §4.

6.2 Complex clauses and subordination

Castro's "The Complex Clause in Boruca" (2010) provides the fullest syntactic treatment in the corpus, organized around the classic parataxis/hypotaxis distinction (following Lehmann 1988).

Paratactic (coordinate) constructions:

  • Asyndetic (juxtaposed, no connective): clauses may share no elements (Káhwik Kwasráng ki ká o-krá yo brú' ki. Kak ki ro'-krá. 'Far from Kwasráng, the big rain finished. The sun shined.') or share arguments (overt, or via zero anaphora) or verbs.
  • Syndetic (connective-linked): the four principal connectives are mang, ní, ihchí, iné ('then', roughly 'and/that', 'and', 'and/but'). Boruca shows a strong preference for syndetic over asyndetic coordination. Ní is polyfunctional — it can coordinate clauses (A'd beyáng-r-i-ng, ní át ki i' beyáng-ra i do'sh 'He loves me and I love him too') or subordinate a complement clause (Ramróhk búsh ki kraé'kra ni i' wá' ki baw'íra 'The woman observed that her own child was being born'), with function disambiguated only by context. Iné can express either coordination or adversative contrast ('but'), again context-dependent.

Hypotactic (subordinate) constructions, divided into complement clauses and adverbial clauses:

  • Complement clauses are relatively rare and function as verb objects (most often with verbs of saying/perception); asyndetic (no connector) or syndetic, introduced by ní ('that'). Toward the end of the language's vitality, the Spanish borrowing ke ('that') increasingly replaces native ní — direct textual evidence of ongoing Spanish-contact attrition.
  • Reported speech has two strategies: (a) non-literal indirect speech as an object complement of tek/te 'to say'; (b) quoted/direct speech, most commonly introduced by the postposition ka ('to') in a construction where the main verb of saying is omitted entirely — e.g. Mang i' be ki ka, 'mí'rang ka, mí'rang ka.' 'Then, to his mom: "Go, go."' — an elliptical quotative pattern that is one of Boruca's more distinctive discourse strategies.
  • Indirect questions are typically marked with the Spanish complementizer ke, or (in one text) with Spanish sábe ('who knows') plus the connector ang — again showing heavy Spanish syntactic influence in this domain by the time of documentation.
  • Adverbial clauses are classified into seven types, each with a dedicated (often disappearing/Spanish-replaced) marker:
    1. Conditional: originally marked by angká ('then/however') at the start of the apodosis (Yó ki tru'rá, angká ógé róhk ki ya' sé-ra 'If it rains, then everybody gets wet'), but by the time of these texts frequently replaced or supplemented by Spanish si ('if'), showing a documented three-stage erosion: native marker only → native marker + Spanish si → Spanish si only.
    2. Purposive: implicit (no marker, second verb often bare or with optional infinitive -í) vs. explicit, marked by chá ('for, in order to') clause-initially and (usually) yét ('for') clause-finally — a discontinuous double-marking pattern unusual cross-linguistically; the second marker yét is sometimes dropped, and Spanish a ver si ('to see if') is also attested intruding into this slot.
    3. Causal: originally marked by postposition úge' ('because') clause-finally, or by the compound demonstrative-focus-causal form yabúge' ('for that reason'); increasingly replaced by Spanish porque.
    4. Temporal: néngwahk ('first') for anteriority, ki dengí ('later') for posteriority; but the single most common way to express temporal sequence in running narrative is simple coordination with mang ('then').
    5. Locative: yé' clause-initially with postposition ta (sometimes replaceable by ki) clause-finally; Spanish desde ('from') is also attested intruding.
    6. Simultaneous: í'k ('when') clause-initially with é' clause-finally, though the second marker is often dropped, and Spanish cuándo is also widely attested as a direct substitute.
    7. Causative/reason-giving (a distinct category from causal, glossing roughly as "since"): the adverb iní' ('since') clause-initially, sometimes with do'sh ('like') clause-finally; can combine with a purposive clause in the same sentence.

Castro's overarching finding — echoed structurally throughout the paper — is a clear, textually documented trajectory of Spanish syntactic encroachment onto originally native subordination markers (conditional si, causal porque, complementizer ke, temporal cuándo, locative desde), which is of direct relevance to revival planning (see §8): a grammar aiming to teach "traditional" Boruca subordination needs to consciously select the older native markers over the Hispanicized forms that appear in some of the very same recorded texts.

7. Cross-Chibchan Comparative Notes

Castro, Murillo & Quesada's "Evolución morfosintáctica en las lenguas chibchas" (2009) situates the Boruca tense→aspect shift (§5.2) alongside two parallel morphosyntactic evolutions in sister Chibchan languages, Teribe and Guaymí, arguing all three exemplify a broader tendency toward "morphologization of grammatical relations" — i.e., discourse/pragmatic patterns hardening into obligatory grammatical marking.

7.1 Teribe: from word order to head-marking morphology

Teribe (a close Isthmian-branch relative of Boruca) shows three coexisting transitive clause orders: basic SOV (used to open discourse and anchor/reinforce participant identity); the more frequent OV-s, where -s is now a grammaticalized person-indexing suffix on the verb (used in flowing/continuous discourse); and an inverse construction OVSdë, where the agent appears post-verbally marked by the obviative particle dë (used especially for 3rd-person-acting-on-3rd-person scenarios where the patient outranks the agent in discourse prominence/animacy). The authors argue, using both language-internal morphological evidence and Chibchan-family-external evidence (Pittier & Gagini's 1891 Teribe/Térraba data), that this inverse construction is a reanalyzed former passive construction: an original SOV-active/SV-passive system, in which the passive existed to avoid disallowed sequences of two same-paradigm pronominal forms and to topicalize patients, evolved as free postverbal pronouns cliticized onto the verb and became person-agreement suffixes, with the passive gradually restricted to (and reanalyzed as an inverse marking) situations where a non-discourse-participant (3rd person) acts on another 3rd person. Formal evidence for this includes fossilized 1st/2nd-person-agent "passive-marked" residues (e.g. Ta kimta-ga pãy dë 'You help me', literally using inverse morphology despite a 1st-person patient) and binding-theory diagnostics showing the postverbal (obviative-marked) NP still functions as the syntactic subject despite its surface position.

7.2 Guaymí: emergence of a marked-nominative system

Panamanian Guaymí (per Kopesec 1975 and Quesada Pacheco 2008) shows a canonical ergative-absolutive pattern: the transitive subject (A) is marked with an ergative morpheme (four allomorphs: kägwe, kä, gwe, kwe, conditioned partly by whether the subject is a full NP, pronoun, or null/postverbal), while intransitive subjects (S) and objects (O) are unmarked. Ergative marking is suspended in present-tense and progressive constructions and optional with verbs of speech, command, and thought.

Costa Rican Guaymí, by contrast — based on the authors' own fieldwork and Payne (1982, 1997) — has reorganized this system into a marked-nominative pattern: the same morpheme family (now realized mainly as gwe, with kwe in free variation and postverbal position) marks subjects of both transitive and intransitive clauses (A and S alike), while objects remain unmarked — the mirror-image typological pattern from the Panamanian variety. The historically ergative form kägwe is unknown to Costa Rican speakers, and kä has been reduced to a purely lexical temporal/locative word ('time'/'place'). The authors treat this as evidence of an active, ongoing, geographically-conditioned reanalysis of the same etymological marker into typologically opposite alignment systems on either side of the Costa Rica–Panama border — a striking case study in rapid syntactic divergence within a single language.

7.3 Synthesis: a shared drift toward head-marking

The authors' summary table (their Cuadro 4) frames all three case studies as variants of a single areal/typological tendency toward increased head-marking (indexation of grammatical relations directly on the verb), achieved by different formal means in each language: in Boruca, via suffixation of the focus marker ang and personal pronouns onto finite verbs to express topic continuity (e.g., at+ang > ang '1SG-FOC', which the same paper documents fusing onto verbs, e.g. So' Juana ang ohrí' sodíh-ra 'Doña Juana makes tortillas'); in Teribe, via suffixation of person markers plus the creation of a wholly separate oblique/object pronoun paradigm; in Cuna, via prefixation of person pronouns in the original SOV order. The authors note that Boruca's own drift toward subject prefixation was interrupted by the language's extinction before the process could complete — placing the ki-clitic grammaticalization pathway described in §4 within this broader family-wide typological current, and underscoring that Boruca was mid-transition on multiple independent grammatical dimensions (TAM marking, subject indexing) at the moment documentation stopped.

8. Gaps and Open Questions for Revival Work

This section is an original synthesis assessment (not drawn from a single source) intended to flag, for materials-development purposes, where the documentary record is thin, internally contested, or likely to mislead a learner if taken at face value.

  1. Phonology is the single biggest gap. None of the eight sources is a dedicated phonological study. What exists (a practical-alphabet chart, a tone-harmony footnote, scattered orthographic notes) is not sufficient to build a phonetically accurate pronunciation guide. Anyone building learner materials will need to go outside this corpus — to Quesada Pacheco's Hablemos boruca (1995) teaching grammar, Constenla & Maroto's original description, or any surviving audio of the last semi-speakers — for a real phonemic inventory, stress rules, and tone-sandhi patterning beyond the single verbal-morphology example given here.
  1. The tense-vs-aspect debate is unresolved, not settled. Castro's aspectual reanalysis (§5.2) is well-argued and independently corroborated by Abarca González's frequency data (the near-total absence of -kirá), but it is explicitly framed by its own author as a proposal, not a consensus — the original tense analysis of Constenla & Maroto and Quesada Pacheco remains the majority position in the earlier literature and is the one embedded in existing teaching materials (e.g., Hablemos boruca, whose abbreviations — PrAF "present affirmative," PfAF "preterite affirmative," etc. — assume the older tense framework; note Quesada Pacheco continued using tense-labeled glosses as late as the 2011 causativity paper). A revival curriculum should probably pick one analysis explicitly and say so, rather than silently mixing tense-labeled and aspect-labeled terminology drawn from different sources — the two glossing systems are not directly interchangeable and mixing them will confuse both instructors and any linguistically sophisticated learners.
  1. The infinitive's distribution is acknowledged as unexplained. Castro is explicit that the intermittent appearance of -í/-íh in near-identical contexts has "no consistency" she could identify from the data, and flags a possible phonological conditioning as a hypothesis for future research. This means any pedagogical rule taught for "when to use the infinitive" will necessarily be a simplification not yet grounded in a confirmed grammatical rule.
  1. The adjective suffix -cá is mentioned but never actually described. Quesada Pacheco's semantic hierarchy for adjectives (§3.8) posits a [– process] suffix -cá as the structural counterpart to -rani, and gives a few contrastive examples (ejtcá 'thin (currently)' vs. ejdát 'thin (inherently)'), but the paper never devotes a numbered section to -cá's own semantic range or productivity the way it does for the other six suffixes. This is a clear content gap for a complete adjective-morphology unit.
  1. Grade/intensity morphology is described as actively decaying within living memory. Quesada Pacheco explicitly reports informants confusing the attenuative (-rani) and intensive (-ón/) grades, and interprets reinforced forms like cuín/crasón/ ('very very-resistant') in Maroto's manuscripts as evidence the system was already breaking down generations before the language's final extinction. A revival grammar should probably present this system with an explicit caveat that even the last fluent-ish speakers were inconsistent about it, rather than presenting it as a stable, fully productive paradigm.
  1. Causative productivity claims rest on a very small, non-representative sample. The morphological causative in -óv is attested for only three verbs; the "unique" suprasegmental tonal causative is explicitly described by its own author as lexicalized/non-productive and was rejected by at least one informant for extension to new verbs. Given that the entire causativity study relies on direct elicitation from a dying-language population (per the paper's own methodological note), frequency/productivity claims here should be treated as suggestive rather than statistically robust — unlike the predicate-suffix frequencies in §5.4, which are based on a substantial 1,516-token natural-narrative corpus and are comparatively much more trustworthy as a basis for sequencing.
  1. No genuine ergativity/subject-marking study of Boruca exists in this corpus. The one source with "sujeto y ergativo" in its title (Wilson, undated) turns out to contain no Boruca data whatsoever — it is purely comparative/theoretical and Bribri-focused. This means Boruca's actual argument-alignment system (word order + discourse-conditioned ki/ang/abí clitics, as described in §4 and referenced in Castro 2012's footnote 6) has not received a dedicated, focused syntactic study of the kind Wilson's title promised. This is a significant documentation gap, since argument marking is foundational for teaching sentence construction.
  1. Spanish-contact attrition is pervasive and needs an explicit editorial policy. Castro's complex-clause paper documents, with textual evidence, a real-time replacement of native subordinators (conditional, causal, complementizer, temporal, locative) by their Spanish equivalents within the very corpus of "traditional" narratives used for documentation. A revival grammar drawing example sentences from these same narrative collections (Constenla & Maroto 1979/1986; Quesada Pacheco 1996) will unavoidably encounter mixed native/Spanish-calque sentences and should decide, and state clearly to users, whether it is teaching the older native forms, the attested "living" (Spanish-influenced) forms actually used by the last speakers, or both with commentary — silently presenting one recorded sentence as "correct traditional Boruca" without flagging its Spanish elements risks teaching hispanized forms as if they were the older grammatical norm, or vice versa.
  1. Cross-source terminological inconsistency. The eight sources, spanning nearly two decades of scholarship by overlapping but not identical author teams, use different glossing conventions and even different romanizations for the same forms (e.g., ki vs. quí; -crá vs. -krá; n̈ vs. η for the velar nasal). A synthesis grammar or teaching curriculum will need to standardize orthography and glossing conventions explicitly, since simply pooling examples verbatim (as this document does, for source fidelity) will otherwise present an inconsistent surface appearance to learners. Partially addressed: the glottal stop specifically has now been standardized to the apostrophe (') for this project — see §2.1. The velar nasal, tone marking, and other romanization variants (§9 above) remain open and should be resolved the same way: by checking which notation is already best-attested in the underlying data and in current community usage, rather than defaulting to whichever source happens to be cited last.
  1. No connected-text longer than the single narrative appendix in Castro (2010) is available in this source set. Only "The Complex Clause in Boruca" includes a full interlinear text with free translation (the Wá' yabá legend). This is invaluable and should be mined further for illustrative material, but a single ~250-word text is a thin base for demonstrating natural discourse-level patterns (e.g., how frequently different subordinator types actually co-occur in real storytelling) — supplementing with the fuller Constenla & Maroto (1979/1986) and Quesada Pacheco (1996) primary text collections, which several of these papers draw examples from but do not reproduce in full, would substantially strengthen any revival curriculum's example base.

Word Formation in Boruca

A synthesis of how Boruca actually builds words — from the documented derivational grammar (reference grammar §3–§5, Appendix B) plus a fresh empirical pass over the 2,255-entry vocabulary database. This is a prerequisite study for the neologism framework: before proposing rules for coining new words, we need to know which processes the language demonstrably used, how productively, and on what kind of source material.

1. Why mine the vocabulary database, not just the grammar papers

The reference grammar's suffix inventory (Appendix B) came from a handful of illustrative examples in each grammar paper — enough to establish that a process exists, not enough to show how hard it actually works across the lexicon. The vocabulary database is a different kind of evidence: 2,255 entries compiled from field elicitation and community sources, largely independent of the grammar papers' own example sentences. Patterns that show up repeatedly there are patterns a coinage method can lean on with more confidence.

Headline finding: 443 of 2,255 headwords (19.6%) are multi-word compounds. Compounding, not suffixation, is the single largest word-formation process visible in the corpus by raw count — which reframes the whole neologism question. The -uá 'camera'/'refrigerator' pattern cited earlier is real and productive, but it's actually the smaller of the two major strategies Boruca uses to name new things.

2. Compounding: the dominant process

Compounding in Boruca strongly favors [generic head noun] + [specifier] structure, where a small set of broad, semantically "bleached" nouns function almost like classifiers, combining productively with a specifier (another noun, an adjective, or occasionally a verb) to name a whole category of related concepts. This is directly evidenced, not inferred:

2.1 các ('mundo, sol, tiempo, día, rayo, trueno, cielo' — world/sun/time/day/sky) as a temporal classifier

Compound Gloss
các ba'cáamanecer ('dawn')
các bejdé'to be late (~5pm)'
các bigátnoche ('night')
các bigát bujtámedianoche ('midnight')
các bocrelámpago, nube ('lightning, cloud')
các bujtámediodía ('midday')

Rule visible here: all divisions of the day/sky/weather are built by taking the broad head các and specifying it — a single polysemous root ('world/sun/time/sky') anchors an entire semantic field rather than each concept getting an unrelated root.

2.2 dí' ('water') as a hydrological/liquid classifier

dí' bu's 'confluence point of a river', dí' cajc 'small river/creek', dí' sá 'headwaters', dí' shú'c 'water duck', dí' yu'crá 'well, pool', dí'suá 'goose/waterfowl'. Fifteen distinct compounds attested, covering both natural water features and water-associated animals — dí' functions as a productive classifier for 'anything defined by its relationship to water.'

2.3 turi ('ganado' — cattle) as a livestock classifier

turi bac 'rancher', turi ca' 'cow's milk', turi crabé'íj 'herder/cowboy', turi ramát 'cow (female)', turi sá' 'slaughterhouse', turi sujgrójc 'bull', turi ú 'stable' (ú = 'house', so literally 'cattle-house'). This entire semantic domain — a domain that necessarily post-dates Spanish contact, since cattle are a colonial-era introduction to the Americas — was built by compounding onto a single borrowed-or-adapted head noun, rather than borrowing a separate Spanish word for every cattle-related concept. This is direct historical evidence that Boruca handled a genuinely new domain of concepts (post-contact livestock ranching) through internal compounding, not wholesale lexical borrowing — exactly the kind of precedent a neologism method for modern technology concepts should point to.

2.4 ca'ísh ('cara' — face) as a facial-anatomy classifier

ca'ísh cuá's 'eyelid', ca'ísh cúp 'eye', ca'ísh é'ua 'mask', ca'ísh shá's 'pupil', ca'ísh shíj 'eyelash/eyebrow'. Face-part anatomy is compositionally derived from 'face' + specifier, rather than each part having an unrelated root.

2.5 rán'cra ('field/plantation of —') as a cultivation-site classifier

This is the cleanest, most rule-like pattern in the whole corpus — a closed, fully regular set: bajyrá rán'cra 'cañaveral' (sugarcane field), briduá rán'cra 'bananal' (banana grove), cáu' rán'cra 'cacaotal' (cacao grove), chubú rán'cra 'algodonal' (cotton field), mun rán'cra 'platanal' (plantain grove), rí' rán'cra 'milpa/maizal' (cornfield), surá rán'cra 'barrial' (mud flat). [crop noun] + rán'cra = 'field of [crop]', with no exceptions found in the data. This is as close to a productive derivational rule as anything in the corpus, and a template any new "field of X" coinage could safely reuse.

2.6 abí ('person, people') + adjective → descriptive ethnonym/person-category compounds

abí brít 'black person' (brít 'negro'), abí drubát 'red/colored person; light-skinned non-Spanish-speaker, esp. of Saxon/Germanic origin' (drubát 'red', cf. Appendix A adjective list), abí suát 'white/Hispanic person' (suát 'white'). Notably abí drubát's gloss shows the pattern extending to a genuinely modern ethnic/linguistic distinction (Spanish-speaking vs. non-Spanish-speaking European-descended people) — further evidence this compounding strategy was still productively used to name new social categories encountered after contact, not a frozen archaic pattern.

2.7 A smaller, less certain classifier: bóc / xít

bóc appears in bóc shuán' 'ash-colored squirrel', bóc suát 'nieve' (snow — likely 'white squirrel' used metaphorically/descriptively for an unfamiliar tropical-latitude phenomenon), bóc tuját 'hielo' (ice), bóc xít 'flying squirrel'. xít appears independently in bót xít 'revolver' (bót = tube/weapon root, cf. bót cúp 'blowgun'), bú'xa xít 'a bird species', sibú xít 'pupil of the eye' (lit. plausibly 'eye's small-one'). The pattern is real but the semantic contribution of xít is not confidently resolved from this data alone (possibly 'small, young, quick, offspring-of') — flagged as a genuine open question rather than asserted, since a neologism method should not build on classifier meanings we're not sure of.

4. Valence-changing and argument-structure marking (already documented, now cross-checked)

The causative/anticausative system (reference grammar §5.3) is a word-formation-adjacent process worth keeping in the same frame as compounding and suffixation, since coining a new verb (not just a noun) will usually mean choosing among: bare lexical alternation (no marking), the -óv suffix (attested unproductive, only 3 verbs), analytic auxiliaries (sodíj, ón̈, shún̈ — the productive, currently-preferred strategy), or the tonal alternation (lexicalized, not extendable to new roots per Quesada Pacheco's own informant-rejection test). For neologism purposes, this means new causative verbs should default to the analytic/auxiliary strategy, which is both the most productive attested pattern and the easiest to teach/learn, rather than attempting suffixal or tonal causativization on new roots.

A separate, previously unremarked pattern surfaced in this pass: a number of vocabulary entries show a verb root followed by a parenthetical postposition marking its argument frame — e.g. babán' (úgue') 'to trade (for)', bia (cá) 'to be ashamed (of)', bin' (at) 'to learn (from)', bin' (ca) 'to pull/impel (toward)'. This isn't word-formation in the coinage sense, but it matters for building new verb entries correctly: Boruca verbs are conventionally documented together with their governed postposition, and any new verb coinage should specify this from the outset rather than leaving argument structure implicit.

5. Processes checked for and NOT found

  • Reduplication. No clear cases of root or syllable reduplication turned up in either the grammar papers or a pass over the vocabulary headwords. This is a real (if soft) negative finding, not just an oversight — worth stating explicitly since reduplication is common elsewhere in the Americas and a neologism method should not assume it's available unless it's later found.
  • Prefixation. All documented derivational and inflectional morphology (Appendix B) is suffixal or clitic; the cross-Chibchan comparative paper (reference grammar §7) notes that prefixation of person markers is how Cuna (a Chibchan relative) marks subjects, explicitly contrasting this with Boruca's own suffixing/cliticizing strategy — so the absence of prefixation in Boruca is typologically meaningful, not just a documentation gap.
  • Infixation. No evidence in any source.
  • Blending/clipping (e.g. portmanteau-style word-shortening as in English "smog"). No clear cases; Boruca's strategy for complex concepts is compounding full roots, not blending partial ones.

6. What this means for a future neologism framework

This section summarizes the generative rules this analysis actually supports, ranked by evidential strength — the neologism framework (next step) should build on these in order, rather than starting from the single -uá example that originally motivated this line of work.

  1. Compounding with a semantic-classifier head noun is the strongest, best-attested strategy, and has direct historical precedent for handling genuinely new domains (turi/cattle for the post-contact livestock domain). New technology concepts are good candidates for this pattern if a suitable classifier head can be identified or established (e.g., a general 'tool/device' or 'machine' head noun, if one exists in the fuller dictionary beyond what's in this database — worth checking Quesada Pacheco & Rojas's full 1999 dictionary if accessible).
  2. The rán'cra 'field of —' pattern is a fully regular, closed rule and a good template for demonstrating how transparent Boruca's compounding logic can be to learners.
  3. The -uá instrument/machine-nominalizer suffix is productive and specifically suited to naming devices and technology, with two independent historical precedents (camera/refrigerator/coffee-maker from the grammar papers; the cotton-skein machine from the vocabulary database).
  4. New verbs should be causativized analytically (via sodíj/ón̈/shún̈), and documented together with their governed postposition from the start.
  5. -át is the safe default for new descriptive adjectives; -ón/ and -rani should be used cautiously or avoided for new coinages, since both show signs of reduced or contested productivity even among the last fluent-ish speakers (reference grammar §8, gap 5, now reinforced by -rani's complete absence from the community dictionary).
  6. Do not assume reduplication, prefixation, infixation, or blending are available — nothing in the documentary record supports building new words that way.

Open item before finalizing a coinage methodology: it would be worth checking whether the fuller Quesada Pacheco & Rojas (1999) dictionary (cited throughout the grammar papers but not itself among the files reviewed for this project) contains additional classifier-head nouns beyond các, dí', turi, ca'ísh, and rán'cra — the five found here are almost certainly not exhaustive, just the ones with enough entries in this particular 2,255-word sample to be statistically visible.

Historical Periods of Boruca

Periodizing the sociolinguistic history of Boruca (Brunka) matters for revival work because each stage left a different kind of evidence — raw wordlists, grammatical sketches, terminal-speaker recordings, or only secondary memory — and each stage explains why the language changed the way it did (contact intensity, schooling policy, community attitude). Knowing which period a source belongs to tells revivalists how reliable, how complete, and how "authentic" (pre- vs. post-attrition) that source's forms are. The periodization below is built primarily from Quesada Pacheco (1994), the richest source on the 19th century, supplemented by Castro (2008) for demographic/20th-21st century detail and J. Diego Quesada (2000) for the terminal-speaker/language-death mechanics.

Period 1: Pre-documentation / Early Colonial Contact (pre-1862)

Boruca is first attested only indirectly: general documentation of Costa Rican indigenous languages begins in the late 18th century (Fernández 1892, Lenguas indígenas de Centro América en el siglo XVIII), but no Boruca-specific manuscript survives from before 1862. The Borucas had reportedly been bilingual in Spanish "since the colonial period" (J. Diego Quesada 2000, fn. 6), reflecting early, comparatively cooperative contact with the Spanish — the Boruca are remembered as early allies of the Spaniards, in contrast to other indigenous groups who resisted, which neighboring groups still cite pejoratively as vividores ("hustlers") (Fernández 1886, cited in Quesada 2000). The Fiesta de los Diablitos (masked mock-battles against "the Spanish," still performed every December 31) preserves a folk memory of open conflict predating this accommodation (Castro 2008). No linguistic data exists from this period; it is reconstructed only from ethnohistorical framing in the later sources.

Period 2: First Foreign Documentation / "The 19th-Century Manuscripts" (1862–1896)

This is the best-documented period thanks to Quesada Pacheco (1994). Four foreign amateur investigators, none linguistically trained, recorded Boruca in succession, each improving on the last: J.J. Valentini (1862) produced the first known Boruca vocabulary, 147 entries (manuscript today at the Van Pelt–Dietrich Library, University of Pennsylvania, likely a copy in C.H. Berendt's hand); William Gabb (1875, published 1875/1882 in Fernández's Colección de documentos) compiled a 250-entry Boruca-Spanish vocabulary and is the first to explicitly document tone ("accent") in a related language (bribri) and the first to record language shift in progress; Bernardo Augusto Thiel (1882) produced by far the largest 19th-century vocabulary (1,100 entries), the first to attempt phrases and conjugated verbs and to record toponyms, plus a separate unpublished 93-entry glossary (held at the Archivo Eclesiástico de la Curia Metropolitana) that shows interference from Térraba, suggesting a bilingual informant; and Henri Pittier, who did fieldwork 1892–1896 (published only in 1941) and produced the richest 19th-century corpus — 1,250 lexical entries, toponyms, grammatical example sentences, and three bilingual texts plus two Spanish-only traditional narrations, including a narrative dictated by informant Jesús Sánchez about an 1896 expedition (13–31 March) to the Burica peninsula. Pittier candidly admitted he never mastered spoken Boruca beyond daily-use dialogue (Pittier 1941:7). Crucially, Gabb in 1875 already reported the first documented sign of language attrition: "the Borucas are rapidly acquiring Spanish… in a group of five Borucas, not one could count except in Spanish" (Gabb 1875/1886:405) — showing shift to Spanish was underway at least a generation before compulsory schooling. All four investigators were foreigners (German, French, English/American mother tongues); Quesada Pacheco (1994) notes the near-total absence of Costa Rican national interest in the language during this period. Phonetic/graphic analysis of these manuscripts shows the glottal stop, tone marking, and the /pʃ/~/kʃ/ to /ʃ/ merger were already unstable or in transition by the 1890s, evidence of a language already undergoing internal change, possibly accelerated by contact.

Period 3: Early Scientific and Ethnographic Interest (1900s–1949)

Foreign scholarly attention continued into the early 20th century even as sociopolitical pressure on the language intensified domestically. Walter Lehmann produced a Boruca-language manuscript ("Boruca Sprache," 1908, Ibero-American Institute, Berlin) and an unpublished travel diary (Tagebuch Costa Rica), later synthesizing Central American languages in Zentral-Amerika (1920). Elías Leiva gave a 1908 conference on a journey through the General, Térraba, and Boruca regions (published in Páginas Ilustradas). Pittier's 1892–96 field materials were finally published posthumously-in-effect in 1941 by the Museo Nacional. Doris Stone's ethnography The Boruca of Costa Rica (Peabody Museum, 1949) offered outside anthropological description. Domestically, this period is identified by J. Diego Quesada (2000) as the true turning point for the language's decline: "the universalization of compulsory and free elementary education at the turn of the century" brought non-Indian (si'kwa) teachers into remote Boruca areas who prohibited use of the language in and out of class and physically punished children for speaking it (elderly informants in the 1990s still bore scars from being struck with textbook spines). Quesada Pacheco (1995:13) notes that despite this, a few monolingual Boruca speakers still existed even during the first half of the 20th century.

Period 4: Generational Language Shift and Contraction (1950s–1970s)

This period is sparsely documented directly (no dedicated period sources survive; it is reconstructed from retrospective testimony in Castro 2008 and J. Diego Quesada 2000). The generation that grew up under the repressive school regime of Period 3 — including terminal speaker Don Ernesto González and fluent elder Doña Paulina Leiva — became the "shifting generation": adults who were themselves fluent but, out of fear and internalized shame, raised their own children in Spanish, interrupting normal intergenerational transmission. J. Diego Quesada (2000) characterizes this as "radical death": language shift and death compressed into one or two generations, rather than the slower multi-generational "gradual death" seen elsewhere, meaning the last fluent speakers largely retained full competence (little structural decay) even as the language essentially stopped being passed on. From the 1970s, the Costa Rican Ministry of Public Education (MEP) began indigenous-language revival efforts, but — per prior project research — with limited success in producing new active speakers, consistent with the "damage was already done" assessment in Quesada (2000).

Period 5: Salvage Linguistics and the Terminal-Speaker Era (1979–2000)

A concentrated new wave of scholarly documentation coincided with the language's final active phase. Adolfo Constenla and native speaker/self-taught linguist Espíritu Santo Maroto published Leyendas y tradiciones borucas (1979/1986, Editorial UCR), a grammatical sketch plus bilingual legend corpus; Maroto also left unpublished manuscripts (e.g., a 1973 manuscript referenced by Quesada Pacheco 1994) and worked to teach the language to younger generations. In 1982 the community received a donated broadcasting station, "Radio Cultural Boruca," explicitly intended to air Boruca-language programming as a preservation measure — this attempt failed, and by the 2000s the station broadcast only in Spanish (Castro 2008). Miguel A. Quesada Pacheco, a non-native fluent speaker, became the dominant researcher of this period: he published the pedagogical grammar Hablemos boruca (1995, MEP/Departamento de Educación Indígena), the narrative collection Narraciones borucas (1996), an article on the Boruca adjective (1997–98), and, with Carmen Rojas, a Boruca-Spanish/Spanish-Boruca dictionary (1999); Quesada Pacheco (1997–98) reported roughly 5 fluent speakers and ~30 semi-speakers in the mid-1990s. J. Diego Quesada's case study "Synopsis of a Boruca Terminal Speaker" (written 1997, published Amerindia 25, 2000) documents this endpoint concretely: the Boruca district held roughly 2,500 people, a dozen passive understanders, and six elderly active speakers, ranging from Doña Paulina Leiva (fluent, linguistically vigorous in her 90s, later helped translate the Gospel of John) to terminal speaker Don Ernesto González, whose regular Boruca use ceased after his wife (a fluent speaker) died in 1984; his speech by the late 1990s showed severe discourse-level breakdown (code-switching into Spanish, referential incoherence) despite intact syntax and phonology. Don Ernesto died in 1998. Espíritu Santo Maroto's own manuscripts were posthumously edited and published by Quesada Pacheco as Lengua o dialecto boruca (1999).

Period 6: Native-Speaker Extinction and the Semi-Speaker Twilight (2001–2010)

The last fully fluent native generation died out in this decade. Per prior project findings (corroborated by Castro 2008, written from a 2007 conference paper): the last fully native speaker died in 2003. By 2007, Castro documented only three semi-speakers remaining — Don Nemesio González (82), Doña Eligia Maroto (75, married to Nemesio, which had helped the couple keep the language in practical use), and Doña Angela González (74, described by the other two as not speaking "correctly"). Doña Eligia died on 21 August 2007, shortly after Castro's fieldwork — this matches the previously known fact that the spouse of the last remaining semi-speaker died in 2007. Castro (2008) documents demographic context via Solano (2000): the Boruca and Curré reserves held 2,017 indigenous inhabitants (1,386 in Boruca, 631 in Curré; 3,936 total including non-indigenous residents), with 48.8% of reserve residents lacking indigenous ancestry — a fact Borucas themselves cite as a cause of language loss. Sociolinguistically, Boruca had narrowed to a "secret code" function: elderly semi-speakers used it to communicate covertly in front of outsiders (e.g., warning a community member during an attempted robbery), and a handful of specific words (e.g., for bathroom needs) persisted among young children as an in-group code, without broader competence. Elementary schools offered Boruca classes, but instruction stopped at the secondary level, and English was increasingly prioritized because of tourism revenue from Boruca crafts. Applying Bauman's (1980) five-point vitality scale, Castro (2008) classifies Boruca as flatly extinct — negative on every vitality indicator, with (semi-)speakers constituting only 0.03% of the total population. Craftsmanship (weaving, masks) rather than language had become the primary marker of Boruca identity by this point.

Period 7: Post-Extinction and the Digital Revival Movement (2010–present, 2026)

None of the three academic source documents (all pre-2010) directly cover this period, so most of the trend-line reasoning below is extrapolated from the trajectory documented in Periods 5–6 and should be treated as inference. However, social-media evidence gathered directly for this project (reviewed with the project owner, 2020–2025) confirms that a real, organized revival effort now exists and gives this period a documentary basis the earlier academic sources lack. Key attested developments: Patrimonio Cultural de Boruca, a Facebook page explicitly framed around cultural/linguistic patrimony, was active by June 2020, publicly mourning the death of Doña Celedina Maroto — described as a "maestra de idioma BORUCA" (teacher of the Boruca language) and praised for her "gran aporte cultural" and fight "por no dejar que se pierda el idioma BRUN"KAJC" ('so that the Brunkajc language is not lost') — direct evidence that dedicated language teachers were still active as late as 2020, a generation after Period 6's extinction assessment. A community Facebook comment thread from around 2020 (on a post by Gilbert González Maroto) shows fluent-register, vocabulary-dense Boruca-Spanish code-switching in ordinary casual conversation (Edixon Mora's comment, referencing cuchumbo, yubú', dibícun xít, guacho, chocao, muasenca), suggesting the language persists at least as an active lexical/identity register among some community members, even if not as a fully spoken first language. By June 2023, community mourning posts (e.g., for Yesenia Mora Lázaro, described as a "gran mujer de Lucha") continued to use Boruca self-identifiers and group names ("Cagrú'rójc Bruncájc"). Most significantly, an organized language-revival collective, Instagram account muntabrunkajctegat ("Colectivo de lengua brunca"), was active as of mid-2025 with the explicit slogan "Nuestro idioma está vivo, los seres de ceniza estamos vivos" ('Our language is alive, the ash-beings are alive') — its content includes archival maps, language-class recordings, and materials for Día Internacional de la Lengua Materna (International Mother Language Day, 21 February) events on technology's role in multilingual learning. Cultural-heritage organizations (e.g., Alma Tica, July 2026) continue to publicize related traditional-arts preservation (mask-carving workshops under Leonel Gómez's "Cró Coñát" initiative), framing craft transmission and language transmission as parts of the same preservation project — continuing the Period 6 pattern (Castro 2008) of craftsmanship functioning alongside language as a marker of Boruca identity, but now explicitly networked with renewed language-specific activism rather than substituting for it. Confirming the current formal status of MEP curriculum, and whether any elderly rememberers/semi-speakers survive as of 2026, would still require sources this research pass did not have access to — but the digital-community evidence establishes that Boruca's post-extinction status is not simply passive heritage memory; there is active, self-organized revival demand, which is the central premise this project (and its companion orthography-standardization work in the reference grammar, §2.1) is built to support.

Summary Timeline

  • Pre-18th century – colonial contact; Borucas noted as early Spanish allies; Fiesta de los Diablitos memorializes prior armed resistance (Fernández 1886, via Quesada 2000)
  • Late 18th century – earliest general documentation of Costa Rican indigenous languages begins (Fernández 1892)
  • 1862 – J.J. Valentini's Boruca vocabulary (147 entries), the first Boruca-specific manuscript (Quesada Pacheco 1994)
  • 1875 – William Gabb's vocabulary (250 entries); first documented report of language shift to Spanish (Gabb 1875/1886, in Quesada Pacheco 1994)
  • 1882 – Bernardo A. Thiel's vocabulary (1,100 entries) plus unpublished 93-entry glossary with Térraba interference (Quesada Pacheco 1994)
  • 1892–1896 – Henri Pittier's fieldwork; largest 19th-century corpus (1,250 entries, texts, toponyms) (published 1941)
  • 13–31 March 1896 – Jesús Sánchez's narrated expedition to the Burica peninsula, recorded by Pittier
  • Early 1900s – universal compulsory elementary education introduced; onset of school-based repression of Boruca (J. Diego Quesada 2000)
  • 1908 – Walter Lehmann's "Boruca Sprache" manuscript and Costa Rica travel diary; Elías Leiva's conference on a journey through Boruca territory
  • 1920 – Lehmann, Zentral-Amerika (Die Sprachen Zentral-Amerikas)
  • 1941 – Pittier's 1892–96 materials finally published by the Museo Nacional
  • First half of 20th c. – a few monolingual Boruca speakers still reported (Quesada Pacheco 1995:13)
  • 1949 – Doris Stone, The Boruca of Costa Rica (Peabody Museum)
  • 1973 – Espíritu Santo Maroto's unpublished manuscript (cited in Quesada Pacheco 1994)
  • 1970s – MEP begins indigenous-language revival efforts (limited success producing active speakers)
  • 1979/1986 – Constenla & Maroto, Leyendas y tradiciones borucas published
  • 1982 – Community receives donated broadcasting station ("Radio Cultural Boruca"); Boruca-language broadcasting attempt fails
  • 1984 – Don Ernesto González's wife dies; his regular Boruca use ends thereafter
  • 1995 – Quesada Pacheco publishes Hablemos boruca teaching grammar (MEP)
  • 1996 – Quesada Pacheco publishes Narraciones borucas
  • 1997 – J. Diego Quesada's terminal-speaker study originally written (for ICA Quito; unattended)
  • 1997–98 – Quesada Pacheco reports ~5 fluent speakers and ~30 semi-speakers
  • 1998 – Terminal speaker Don Ernesto González dies
  • 1999 – Quesada Pacheco & Rojas, Boruca-Spanish dictionary; Maroto's manuscripts posthumously published
  • 2000 – J. Diego Quesada, "Synopsis of a Boruca Terminal Speaker" published (Amerindia 25); ~6 elderly active speakers, dozen passive understanders in a district of ~2,500
  • 2003 – Last fully native (fluent) speaker of Boruca dies
  • September 2007 – Castro presents fieldwork at I Simposio ROLIBCA, Universidad Nacional; only 3 semi-speakers documented
  • 21 August 2007 – Doña Eligia Maroto (semi-speaker) dies
  • 2008 – Castro's article published (Letras 43), classifying Boruca as extinct per Bauman's vitality scale
  • June 2020 – Patrimonio Cultural de Boruca (Facebook) mourns Doña Celedina Maroto, an active Boruca-language teacher, evidencing continued language teaching a generation after the extinction assessment
  • ~2020 – community Facebook comment thread shows vocabulary-dense, fluent-register Boruca-Spanish code-switching in casual conversation
  • June 2023 – community mourning posts continue using Boruca self-identifiers and group names ("Cagrú'rójc Bruncájc")
  • By mid-2025 – Instagram collective muntabrunkajctegat ("Colectivo de lengua brunca") active, running language classes and Día de la Lengua Materna programming, slogan "Nuestro idioma está vivo"
  • July 2026 – Alma Tica publicizes the "Cró Coñát" mask-carving/language-culture preservation initiative (Leonel Gómez)
  • 2010s–2026 – formal MEP curriculum continuity beyond the 1970s program, and whether any elderly rememberers/semi-speakers survive, remain unconfirmed by available sources; digital-community evidence, however, confirms active, self-organized revival demand rather than passive heritage memory alone
